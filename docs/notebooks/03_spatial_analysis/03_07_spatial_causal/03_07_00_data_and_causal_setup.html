Spatial Causal Inference

Why semi-synthetic? With real observational data the true causal effect is unknown, so we could never tell whether a method worked. We therefore keep the real covariates, the real geography and the real 2024 adult diabetes prevalence, and simulate a hypothetical treatment and its effect on top. Confounding patterns, county shapes and neighbour structure are realistic, and the truth is known. Notebooks 04 and 08 also analyse genuinely real relationships, where no ground truth exists.

Nine notebooks estimate a causal effect when the units are counties that share borders. The running example is a hypothetical community-health-worker program and adult diabetes prevalence in 3,109 contiguous U.S. counties.

  • Spatial Causal Inference. This notebook. Potential outcomes, the county data, spatial weights, and the semi-synthetic design.
  • Spatial Confounding. A smooth spatial trend that looks like a treatment effect. OLS with a smooth, spatial+, spatial double machine learning, and Conley standard errors.
  • Interference and Spillovers. Direct, spillover, and total effects when a treated neighbour changes the outcome. Exposure mappings, AIPW, and Hájek.
  • Spatially Varying Treatment Effects. A county-level effect that is larger where child poverty is high. A DR-learner, geographically weighted DML, and a geographically weighted causal forest.
  • Geographic Regression Discontinuity. The effect at a 2024 Medicaid-expansion border. An Imbens-Kalyanaraman bandwidth, segment fixed effects, and the real uninsured share.
  • Panel Designs: DiD with Spillovers, Staggered Adoption, Synthetic Control. Difference-in-differences with spillover rings, staggered adoption, and spatial synthetic control, 2014 to 2025.
  • Bayesian Spatial Causal Models (ICAR and BYM). ICAR and BYM, a collapsed Gibbs sampler, and a Poisson BYM for premature-death counts.
  • Designing Spatial Experiments. Cluster and saturation designs, GRTS, rerandomisation, and power for a trial in seven southern states.
  • Spatial Causal Discovery. Spatial Granger, geographical convergent cross mapping, and NOTEARS, including real county health measures.

Overview

Spatial causal inference asks what would have happened to an outcome in a place if a treatment there, or in the places around it, had been different. The units in this chapter are counties. The outcome is adult diabetes prevalence. The treatment, in the worked examples, is a hypothetical community-health-worker program. The effect in a county is the difference between the prevalence that county would have had with the program and the prevalence it would have had without it. Only one of those two prevalences is ever observed. The other has to be constructed from other counties, other years, or a model of how the program was assigned.

A non-spatial analysis can already do that construction when three things hold. A county’s outcome depends only on its own treatment. Every common cause of the program and of diabetes is measured. Treated and untreated counties both appear at every combination of those measured causes. County data break all three, because the counties sit on a map.

A treated neighbour changes your outcome. A program, a clinic, or a pollutant does not stop at the county line. People commute, share a media market, and use services in the next county. The usual assumption that unit \(i\) responds only to its own treatment then fails. The effect splits. The direct effect is the change in county \(i\) from treating county \(i\). The spillover is the change in county \(i\) from treating its neighbours. The total effect is the sum. Notebooks 02 and 05 estimate those pieces. Notebook 07 designs a trial so the spillover can be measured on purpose, with buffers and saturation, rather than discovered after the fact.

The reasons a county is treated are themselves a map. Poverty, rurality, and state policy vary smoothly. Two counties with the same measured covariates can still differ by an unmeasured surface \(U(s)\): a regional climate of policy, an environmental exposure, a labour market. If that surface also raises diabetes, a plain comparison of counties with and without the program attributes the geography to the program. That is spatial confounding. Adjusting for a list of covariates does not remove a surface that was never measured. Notebook 01 fits the smooth part of the map and then estimates the program in what remains, with spatial+, spatial double machine learning, and standard errors that allow neighbouring residuals to be correlated. Notebook 06 puts a prior on that surface with an intrinsic CAR or a BYM model.

The effect need not be one number. A program can matter more where child poverty is high, or only in the Deep South, or only in the strip of counties that touch a state border. Notebook 03 maps the effect. Notebook 04 uses a real policy border, the 2024 Medicaid-expansion line, as a geographic regression discontinuity: counties just inside and just outside the line are compared, and the bandwidth decides how far “just” reaches. Notebook 05 follows the same counties from 2014 to 2025, so a county can be its own comparison across years, including when adoption is staggered and when neighbours are treated later.

Neighbouring counties are not separate draws. Even after the effect is identified, the residuals of adjacent counties move together. A standard error that treats 3,109 counties as independent observations is too small, and a confidence interval built from it is too narrow. Conley standard errors and spatial-block resampling keep clusters of neighbours together. The same dependence is what notebook 08 tries to learn rather than adjust away: which county health measures point at which, using spatial Granger causality, geographical convergent cross mapping, and a NOTEARS model with spatial-lag nodes.

Section 1 writes the two potential outcomes, the average effect, and the three assumptions in notation. The semi-synthetic design later in this notebook keeps the real county covariates, the real boundaries, and the real 2024 diabetes map, and lays a known program effect on top, so each of those methods can be checked against a number we chose.

Potential outcomes

For county \(i\) with binary treatment \(T_i \in \{0,1\}\) we imagine two potential outcomes: \(Y_i(1)\) (outcome if treated) and \(Y_i(0)\) (outcome if not). The unit-level effect is \(\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\), but only one potential outcome is ever observed:

\[Y_i = T_i\,Y_i(1) + (1-T_i)\,Y_i(0).\]

Common estimands:

\[\text{ATE} = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)], \qquad \text{ATT} = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid T=1], \qquad \tau(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X=x]\ \text{(CATE)}.\]

Identification from observational data rests on three assumptions:

  1. SUTVA: no interference (\(Y_i\) depends only on \(T_i\), not on \(T_j\)) and one version of treatment.
  2. Unconfoundedness: \(\{Y(0),Y(1)\} \perp T \mid X\): all common causes of \(T\) and \(Y\) are measured.
  3. Positivity: \(0 < e(x) = P(T=1\mid X=x) < 1\).

Then \(\text{ATE} = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid T=1,X] - \mathbb{E}[Y\mid T=0,X]\big]\).

How space breaks each assumption

Assumption Spatial violation Notebook
SUTVA a program in one county also helps its neighbours (interference) 02, 05
Unconfoundedness an unmeasured, spatially smooth factor \(U(s)\) (regional policy climate, environment) drives both treatment and outcome (spatial confounding) 01, 06
Independence for inference outcomes of neighbouring counties are correlated, so i.i.d. standard errors are too small 01
Constant effect effects differ across regions (spatial heterogeneity) 03

Spatial weights and spatial autocorrelation

A spatial weights matrix \(W\) encodes who neighbours whom. With Queen contiguity \(w_{ij}=1\) if counties \(i\) and \(j\) share a boundary point. Row-standardising, \(\tilde w_{ij} = w_{ij}/\sum_k w_{ik}\), turns the spatial lag

\[(Wy)_i = \sum_j \tilde w_{ij}\, y_j\]

into the average of the neighbours’ values. Moran’s I measures global spatial autocorrelation:

\[I = \frac{n}{S_0}\,\frac{\sum_i\sum_j w_{ij}(y_i-\bar y)(y_j-\bar y)}{\sum_i (y_i-\bar y)^2}, \qquad S_0=\sum_{ij} w_{ij}.\]

\(I>0\) means similar values cluster. Inference uses random permutations of \(y\) over locations. Weights and Moran’s I come from geolattice.spatial_statistics.

Code 
from geolattice.spatial_statistics.esda import Moran

w = cc.queen_weights()
card = w.cardinalities if not callable(w.cardinalities) else w.cardinalities()
isl = w.islands if not callable(w.islands) else w.islands()
print(f"Queen weights: mean {np.mean(card):.2f} neighbours; islands: "
      f"{gdf.loc[isl, 'county'].tolist()} ({gdf.loc[isl, 'state_abbr'].tolist()})")

rows = []
for v in ["diabetes_pct", "obesity_pct", "inactivity_pct", "child_poverty_pct", "log_income", "uninsured_pct"]:
    m = Moran(gdf[v].to_numpy(), w, permutations=199, seed=1)
    rows.append({"variable": v, "Moran's I": round(m.I, 3), "z": round(m.z_score, 1), "p (perm.)": m.p_value})

Every variable is strongly spatially autocorrelated (\(I \approx 0.6\)-\(0.75\); \(p=0.005\) is the smallest value 199 permutations can give). Any omitted cause of diabetes is therefore likely to be spatially smooth too, which is exactly the setting where spatial confounding arises.

The semi-synthetic causal design (Scenario A)

We imagine a county community health worker (CHW) diabetes program. Counties choose to run it; we observe \(T_i\) and adult diabetes prevalence \(Y_i\) (percentage points).

Unmeasured confounder. \(U(s)\) is a smooth random surface (length scale 350 km, standardised) standing for an unobserved “regional health-policy climate”. It is not among the covariates.

Treatment assignment (logistic):

\[e_i = P(T_i=1\mid X_i,U_i)=\operatorname{expit}\!\big(-0.55+0.5\,z(\text{child pov.})_i+0.4\,z(\text{uninsured})_i -0.3\,z(\text{rural})_i+0.3\,z(\log\text{PCP})_i+1.0\,U_i\big).\]

Potential outcomes. With \(D_i\) the real 2024 diabetes prevalence,

\[Y_i(0) = D_i + 0.9\,U_i, \qquad \tau_i = -0.8-0.35\,z(\text{child pov.})_i-0.6\,\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{\lVert s_i-s_{\text{Jackson,MS}}\rVert^2}{2\cdot 400^2}\Big),\]

so the program lowers diabetes prevalence more in poorer counties and in the Deep South.

Spillover. Outreach crosses county lines: each county also gains \(\delta\cdot\bar T_{N(i)}\) with \(\delta=-0.4\) and \(\bar T_{N(i)}=(WT)_i\) the share of treated Queen neighbours:

\[Y_i = Y_i(0)+\tau_i T_i+\delta\,(WT)_i.\]

We store both y (with spillover) and y_noint (without), so notebook 01 can study confounding alone and notebook 02 adds interference.

Three different “true effects” exist because effects are heterogeneous:

  • ATE \(=\frac1n\sum_i\tau_i\): the average over all counties.
  • ATT: the average over treated counties (larger, because treated counties are poorer).
  • PLM estimand \(=\sum_i e_i(1-e_i)\tau_i \big/ \sum_i e_i(1-e_i)\): what a partially linear model (DML, notebook 01) estimates when effects vary: an overlap-weighted average. We can compute it because we know the true propensity \(e_i\).

The naive comparison

The simple difference in means compares treated with untreated counties: \(\hat\Delta = \bar Y_{T=1}-\bar Y_{T=0}\). Because \(U\) raises both the chance of treatment and the outcome, \(\hat\Delta\) is pushed upward, away from the (negative) true effect.

Code 
naive = gA.loc[gA["T"] == 1, "y_noint"].mean() - gA.loc[gA["T"] == 0, "y_noint"].mean()
print(f"naive difference in means: {naive:+.3f}   true ATT: {truth['att']:+.3f}   true ATE: {truth['ate']:+.3f}")
print(f"correlation of T with U: {np.corrcoef(gA['T'], gA['U'])[0, 1]:.2f};  with real diabetes D: "
      f"{np.corrcoef(gA['T'], gA['diabetes_pct'])[0, 1]:.2f}")
naive difference in means: +0.663   true ATT: -0.978   true ATE: -0.890
correlation of T with U: 0.41;  with real diabetes D: 0.19

The naive estimate has the wrong sign: the program appears to raise diabetes, because treated counties are poorer and sit where \(U\) is high. Notebook 01 shows what does and does not remove this bias.
