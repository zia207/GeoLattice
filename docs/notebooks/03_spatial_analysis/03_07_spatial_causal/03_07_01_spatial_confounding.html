Spatial Confounding¶

This notebook estimates the effect of a community-health-worker program on adult diabetes prevalence when an unmeasured, spatially smooth factor drives both adoption and diabetes. The data are Scenario A without spillovers (y_noint), built in notebook 00. The true average treatment effect is $-0.890$. The overlap-weighted estimand that a partially linear model targets is $-0.907$.

Diagnostics use Moran's I. The adjustments are OLS with a spatial smooth, spatial+, and spatial double machine learning, with Conley and spatial-block standard errors. The functions are diagnose_spatial_confounding, SpatialPlus, and SpatialDML in geolattice.spatial_analysis.spatialcausal.

Why an omitted smooth confounder biases regression¶

Suppose the outcome model is $Y_i=\alpha+\tau T_i+X_i^\top\beta+\gamma U_i+\varepsilon_i$ and we regress $Y$ on $(T,X)$ without $U$. By the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem the OLS coefficient is

$$\hat\tau_{\text{OLS}}=\frac{\sum_i \tilde T_i Y_i}{\sum_i \tilde T_i^2} \;\xrightarrow{p}\;\tau+\gamma\,\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\tilde T,\tilde U)}{\operatorname{Var}(\tilde T)},$$

where $\tilde T$ and $\tilde U$ are the residuals of $T$ and $U$ after regressing on $X$. The bias is the product of the confounder's effect on $Y$ ($\gamma$) and its association with treatment that $X$ does not explain. In our design $\gamma=0.9$ and $U$ enters the propensity, so the bias is large and positive.

Diagnostics¶

If the treatment residual $\tilde T$ is spatially autocorrelated, a smooth omitted variable has room to bias the estimate. If the outcome residual is autocorrelated too, the risk is real, and i.i.d. standard errors will also be wrong. diagnose_spatial_confounding computes Moran's I (via geolattice.spatial_statistics) for $T$, $Y$ and both residuals.

Adding a spatial smooth, and why that is not enough¶

A natural fix is to add a spatial basis $S$ (functions of location) to the regression,

$$Y_i=\alpha+\tau T_i+X_i^\top\beta+S_i^\top\theta+\varepsilon_i,$$

or a spatial random effect (notebook 06). Two bases are used here.

  • Moran eigenvector maps (Griffith; Dray et al.): eigenvectors of $MCM$ with $M=I-\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top/n$ and $C=(W+W^\top)/2$. Ordered by eigenvalue, they run from the broadest positively autocorrelated patterns to finer ones.
  • Radial basis functions centred on $k$ k-means knots $c_m$: $S_{im}=\exp\!\big(-\lVert s_i-c_m\rVert^2/2h^2\big)$.

By FWL, $\hat\tau$ now uses only the part of $T$ that is orthogonal to $X$ and $S$. If the basis is rich enough to span $U$, the bias disappears; if not, the remaining confounding stays. Hodges & Reich (2010) and Paciorek (2010) add a subtler point. With a penalised smooth or random effect, the smooth and the treatment compete for the same large-scale variation, and the estimate can move in either direction. Adding "space" is not automatically a confounding adjustment.

spatial+¶

Dupont, Wood & Augustin (2022) remove the spatially smooth part of the treatment first:

  1. Regress $T$ on a rich basis $S_t$: $\;T_i=S_{t,i}^\top\gamma+\tilde T_i$.
  2. Fit $Y_i=\alpha+\tau\tilde T_i+X_i^\top\beta+f(s_i)+\varepsilon_i$, with $f$ a penalised smooth.

The coefficient on $\tilde T$ is identified from treatment variation that is spatially rough, which a smooth confounder cannot explain. In geolattice.spatial_analysis.spatialcausal step 1 uses $k_t=\max(3k,\,n/10)$ unpenalised Moran eigenvectors and step 2 a ridge-penalised basis with the penalty chosen by generalised cross-validation.

Spatial double machine learning¶

The partially linear model¶

$$Y=\theta\,T+g(X,s)+\varepsilon,\qquad T=m(X,s)+v,\qquad \mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X,s,T]=0,\ \mathbb E[v\mid X,s]=0.$$

Here $g$ and $m$ are unknown functions learned by machine learning. Including location $s$ (coordinates, spatial lags, basis functions) lets $g$ and $m$ absorb a smooth confounder.

Neyman-orthogonal score and cross-fitting¶

Write $\ell(X,s)=\mathbb E[Y\mid X,s]$. DML solves the sample analogue of $\mathbb E[\psi]=0$ with the score

$$\psi(W;\theta,\ell,m)=\big(Y-\ell(X,s)-\theta\,(T-m(X,s))\big)\,\big(T-m(X,s)\big),$$

giving the residual-on-residual estimator

$$\hat\theta=\frac{\sum_i \hat v_i\,\hat u_i}{\sum_i \hat v_i^2},\qquad \hat u_i=Y_i-\hat\ell(X_i,s_i),\quad \hat v_i=T_i-\hat m(X_i,s_i).$$

The score is Neyman orthogonal: its derivative with respect to the nuisance functions is zero at the truth, so first-order errors in $\hat\ell,\hat m$ do not bias $\hat\theta$. Cross-fitting predicts each $\hat\ell(X_i),\hat m(X_i)$ from models trained on other folds, which removes overfitting bias. Under heterogeneous effects $\tau(x,s)$ the PLM estimand is the overlap-weighted average

$$\theta_{\text{PLM}}=\frac{\mathbb E\big[\operatorname{Var}(T\mid X,s)\,\tau(X,s)\big]}{\mathbb E\big[\operatorname{Var}(T\mid X,s)\big]} =\frac{\mathbb E[e(1-e)\,\tau]}{\mathbb E[e(1-e)]}\quad\text{(binary }T),$$

which is why notebook 00 computed that quantity as the target.

Spatial cross-fitting and its trade-off¶

With spatial dependence a neighbour in the training fold leaks information about a held-out county, so SpatialDML holds out spatial blocks (k-means clusters of locations). Block size involves a real trade-off that this dataset makes vivid:

  • blocks should be larger than the range of residual dependence (to stop leakage), but
  • smaller than the scale of any confounder the nuisance models must learn from location. A held-out block as large as the confounder's range forces the models to extrapolate $U$ into a region they never saw, and the confounding comes back.

The basis also has to be rich enough to represent a surface that varies every ~350 km across a 4,600 km domain. geolattice.spatial_analysis.spatialcausal therefore scales both defaults with $n$: $\max(25,\ n/15)$ blocks and $\min(200,\ \max(30,\ n/20))$ radial basis functions. These defaults were revised in response to exactly this analysis.

Reading the table:

  • Without location features DML cannot see $U$ and inherits the OLS bias.
  • Raw coordinates and spatial lags barely help a linear nuisance model: coordinates enter linearly, and a plane cannot represent a surface with several hills and valleys.
  • A rich RBF basis with auto (small) blocks or random folds recovers the truth (bias ≈ 0.01).
  • The same basis with 25 large blocks overshoots (−1.10, with a much larger SE): each held-out region must be predicted from basis coefficients fitted elsewhere, and those extrapolations are poor. Block size changes the answer, not just the standard error.
  • The random forest with coordinates and RBFs is less accurate than ridge here, because a smooth surface is easier for a linear model on a smooth basis than for trees.

Standard errors under spatial dependence¶

All estimators in the package are asymptotically linear, $\hat\theta-\theta\approx\frac1n\sum_i\psi_i$, so a standard error only needs the influence contributions $\psi_i$:

  • i.i.d.: $\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\theta)=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_i\psi_i^2$.
  • Conley (1999) spatial HAC: keep covariances between pairs closer than a cutoff $d^*$, down-weighted by a kernel, $$\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{\text{C}}(\hat\theta)=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_i\sum_j K\!\Big(\frac{d_{ij}}{d^*}\Big)\psi_i\psi_j,\qquad K(u)=(1-u)_+\ \text{(Bartlett)}.$$
  • Block (cluster-robust): treat spatial blocks $b$ as clusters, $\frac{G}{G-1}\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_b\big(\sum_{i\in b}\psi_i\big)^2$.

The cutoff is a modelling choice. suggest_cutoff reads it off the residual correlogram, and a sensitivity curve shows how much it matters.

Summary and Conclusions¶

  1. A spatially smooth unmeasured confounder can flip the sign of a naive estimate and attenuate a covariate-adjusted one by ~70%.
  2. "Adding space" works only if the spatial terms can actually represent the confounder. spatial+ achieves this by removing the smooth part of the treatment; DML achieves it with a rich basis.
  3. Spatial cross-fitting needs blocks that are small relative to the confounder's scale; large blocks bring the bias back through extrapolation.
  4. Report spatially robust standard errors and show their sensitivity to the cutoff.

No method can adjust for a confounder that is not spatially smooth and not measured; spatial methods only help with the smooth part.
