Spatial Analysis

Spatial analysis is the cornerstone of Geographic Information Science (GIS). It encompasses a wide family of computational and statistical methods that extract meaning from the location, shape, pattern, and relationship of geographic features. Unlike conventional data analysis, which treats observations as independent, spatial analysis explicitly accounts for the fact that nearby things tend to be more similar (Tobler’s First Law of Geography) and that where something occurs is often as important as what it is.

This notebook is the entry point for Module 03 of GeoLattice. It explains what spatial analysis is, why it matters, and provides a structured overview of every sub-module in this section.

Eight parts take a layer from a measurement to a mapped decision, using geolattice.spatial_analysis.

  • Spatial Analysis. This notebook, then Vector Data Analysis (overlay, proximity, measurement) and Raster Data Analysis (map algebra, focal statistics, zonal summaries).

  • Digital Terrain Modeling (DTM). Condition a DEM, derive slope, aspect, curvature, and landforms, and route flow into streams and watersheds.

  • Spatial Analysis of Multidimensional Raster Data. Manage a time cube, aggregate it, map anomalies, fit a trend, and reduce the cube with principal components.

  • Multi-Criteria Suitability Modeling (MCSM). Turn criteria into a suitability map with categorical overlay, weighted overlay, and fuzzy membership, then test how much the weights matter.

  • Spatial Network Analysis. Embed places in a graph, route on OpenStreetMap, build a network from GeoPandas, and map service areas and flows.

  • Segregation Analysis. Measure how unevenly groups share a city, from a single index to local, multiscale, and inferential tests.

  • Spatial Causal Inference. Estimate what a county’s outcome would have been if a treatment there, or next door, had been different: confounding, spillovers, effects that vary across the map, and a trial designed for interference.

What is Spatial Analysis?

Spatial analysis is the process of examining the locations, attributes, and relationships of features in spatial data to discover patterns, understand phenomena, and make decisions. It integrates principles from geography, mathematics, statistics, and computer science to answer questions such as:

  • Where are things located, and how do they cluster or disperse?
  • What is the relationship between spatial features (overlap, adjacency, proximity)?
  • How do terrain, hydrology, or land cover influence human and ecological processes?
  • Which areas are most suitable for a specific use?
  • How does a phenomenon change over space and time?

The term was popularised by geographers and quantitative researchers in the 1960s-70s (most notably by Cliff & Ord on spatial autocorrelation, and later by Anselin on exploratory spatial data analysis) and has since grown into a multidisciplinary field underpinning environmental science, urban planning, public health, logistics, remote sensing, and more.

Branches of Spatial Analysis

Branch Description Example
Spatial query & selection Filter features by location or attributes All hospitals within 10 km of a flood zone
Measurement Distance, area, perimeter, shape metrics Euclidean vs network distance between cities
Overlay analysis Combine layers to identify intersections Crop suitability = soil ∩ slope ∩ rainfall
Proximity analysis Buffers, Voronoi polygons, nearest-neighbour Service area of a clinic
Terrain / surface analysis Slope, aspect, hillshade, viewshed Landslide hazard from DEM
Hydrological analysis Flow routing, watershed delineation Catchment area above a dam site
Pattern analysis Clustering, hotspots, spatial autocorrelation Disease cluster detection
Network analysis Routing, accessibility, flow Shortest evacuation route
Suitability / MCSM Multi-criteria decision support Best site for a wind farm
Spatio-temporal analysis Change detection, trend analysis NDVI trends from time-series satellite data

Why Spatial Analysis Matters

Location matters in virtually every field of science and applied decision-making:

  • Environmental Science: Understanding erosion, flood risk, habitat connectivity, and climate change impacts.
  • Public Health: Mapping disease outbreaks, identifying underserved populations, optimizing clinic placement.
  • Urban Planning: Site selection, accessibility analysis, land-use zoning, infrastructure routing.
  • Agriculture: Precision farming, crop yield prediction, irrigation planning, soil variability mapping.
  • Disaster Management: Risk mapping, emergency routing, evacuation modelling, damage assessment.
  • Business & Logistics: Trade area analysis, optimal warehouse location, delivery routing.
  • Remote Sensing & Earth Observation: Multi-temporal change detection, time-series trend analysis, anomaly detection.

Python, through libraries such as GeoPandas, Rasterio, xarray, WhiteboxTools, OSMnx, NetworkX, and scikit-fuzzy, has become the dominant open-source platform for spatial analysis. GeoLattice unifies these tools behind a consistent, GIS-practitioner-friendly API organised around the workflows described in this module.

Core Concepts

Before diving into the notebooks, a few foundational concepts are worth reviewing:

Spatial Data Models

Model Description Typical Formats
Vector Discrete objects: points, lines, polygons with attributes Shapefile, GeoJSON, GeoPackage, FlatGeobuf
Raster Continuous grid of cells (pixels) with a value per cell GeoTIFF, NetCDF, HDF5, Cloud-Optimised GeoTIFF
Multidimensional raster Raster with extra axes: time, depth, wavelength NetCDF, HDF5, Zarr
Network / Graph Nodes + edges with spatial embedding GraphML, OSM, GeoPackage

Coordinate Reference Systems (CRS)

All spatial analysis must occur in a consistent CRS. Key distinctions: - Geographic CRS (lon/lat, e.g., WGS 84 / EPSG:4326): angles, not metres: avoid for distance/area calculations. - Projected CRS (e.g., UTM, BTM): metres/feet: required for accurate distance, area, and slope calculations. - GeoLattice uses pyproj and PROJ under the hood; always check .crs before running analysis.

Tobler’s First Law of Geography

“Everything is related to everything else, but near things are more related than distant things.”
Waldo Tobler, 1970

This principle motivates spatial autocorrelation analysis, kriging, and the spatial weight matrices used throughout this module.

Scale and Resolution

Results depend heavily on the spatial resolution of the input data and the analysis scale (the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem. MAUP). Always document data provenance and resolution.

GeoLattice Spatial Analysis Architecture

The geolattice.spatial_analysis sub-package is structured around six functional domains that map directly to the six sub-modules of this tutorial section:

geolattice
└── spatial_analysis
    ├── vector_analysis          # Module 03.01
    ├── terrain_analysis         # Module 03.02
    ├── surface_hydrology        # Module 03.03
    ├── multidimensional         # Module 03.04
    ├── suitability              # Module 03.05
    │   ├── vector_suitability
    │   ├── raster_suitability
    │   └── fuzzy

Each sub-module is designed to be self-contained: you can import and use any part without needing others. Common utilities (CRS handling, plotting styles, data loaders) live in geolattice.utils and geolattice.viz.

Key design principles

Principle What it means in practice
GeoDataFrame-first Vector functions accept and return geopandas.GeoDataFrame
Rasterio / xarray bridge Raster functions work with file paths, rasterio datasets, or xarray.DataArray
WhiteboxTools integration Terrain and hydrology leverage Whitebox under the hood via a thin GeoLattice wrapper
NetworkX / OSMnx integration Network functions work with networkx.Graph / MultiDiGraph objects
Reproducible outputs All functions accept output path arguments and log their Whitebox/GDAL calls

Multi-Criteria Suitability Modeling (MCSM)

API modules: geolattice.spatial_analysis.suitability, geolattice.spatial_analysis.fuzzy

Multi-Criteria Suitability Modeling (MCSM) integrates multiple spatial criteria to identify optimal or suitable locations for a given objective. It is one of the most widely applied decision-support frameworks in GIS: used in site selection, land-use planning, conservation prioritisation, and infrastructure routing.

GeoLattice supports three complementary approaches, each covered by a dedicated notebook series:

Case study: Identifying underserved US counties for healthcare facility siting.

Part II: Fuzzy Suitability Modeling

Classical suitability models use crisp Boolean thresholds: a location either meets a criterion or it does not. Fuzzy logic allows partial membership: a slope of 14° might be “somewhat suitable” (membership = 0.7) rather than simply suitable or not. This is more realistic and reduces the sensitivity to arbitrary threshold choices.

Notebook Focus
03_04_02_00_fuzzy_logic_foundations.ipynb Crisp vs fuzzy sets, ArcGIS-style membership functions (linear, sigmoidal, Gaussian, near, etc.), hedges, overlay operators
03_04_02_01_fuzzy_vector_suitability.ipynb Applying fuzzy memberships and AND/OR/PRODUCT/SUM overlays to New York counties
03_04_02_03_fuzzy_raster_suitability.ipynb Terrain criteria from a 90 m DEM, fuzzification, fuzzy overlay for solar-farm siting
03_04_02_04_advanced_fuzzy_suitability.ipynb Gamma operator, parameter sensitivity (Jaccard similarity), weighted fuzzy operators, Mamdani FIS

Part III: Sensitivity and Uncertainty Analysis

Summary

Module 03 of GeoLattice provides a complete, practitioner-oriented spatial analysis toolkit structured around six domains:

# Sub-module Core Library Primary Data Model
03.01 Vector Data Analysis GeoPandas, Shapely GeoDataFrame (points, lines, polygons)
03.02 Terrain Analysis WhiteboxTools, Rasterio GeoTIFF (DEM)
03.03 Hydrology Modeling WhiteboxTools, Rasterio GeoTIFF (DEM + derived rasters)
03.04 Multidimensional Raster xarray, netCDF4, SciPy xarray.Dataset (NetCDF / Zarr)
03.05 Suitability Modeling GeoPandas, scikit-fuzzy, SALib GeoDataFrame + GeoTIFF
03.06 Network Analysis NetworkX, OSMnx, igraph Graph / MultiDiGraph + GeoDataFrame

All sub-modules share a consistent design philosophy: GeoDataFrame-first, reproducible, and bridged to best-in-class scientific Python libraries. Together they cover the full breadth of spatial analysis workflows needed in environmental science, planning, logistics, and geographic data science.
