Segregation Over Time: Boundaries, Panels, and Decomposition

Overview

Segregation research is mostly about change: did Buffalo integrate, and why? Two technical problems stand between census tables and an answer.

  1. Boundaries move. The Census Bureau redraws tracts every decade: Erie and Niagara counties had about 300 tracts in 2000 and 2010 and 325 in 2020, with many split, merged, or reshaped. Indices computed on different geographies are not comparable (the modifiable areal unit problem, MAUP).
  2. Change has more than one source. An index can fall because people moved (the spatial arrangement changed) or because the metro area’s group mix changed (composition). Policy implications differ, so the two need to be separated.

This notebook follows the PySAL tutorial on decomposition and extends it to a multi-period panel with seg.areal_interpolate, seg.dasymetric_interpolate, seg.harmonize_panel, seg.compute_panel, seg.trend, seg.decompose_change, and seg.chain_decomposition. It also shows how the harmonized panel used in notebooks 03_07_01 to 03_07_06 was built.

Topics:

  • Real tract boundary changes, 2000 to 2020, and the MAUP
  • Areal and dasymetric interpolation with a housing-unit raster from census blocks
  • Validating interpolation against block-level truth
  • A harmonized panel with trend test
  • Shapley decomposition of change into spatial and composition parts, chained across decades
  • Exporting a tract-by-year panel for causal models

Setup

The next cell finds the repository root, puts it on the import path, points DATA at Data/03_spatial_analysis/data_segregation/buffalo, imports geolattice.spatial_analysis.segregation as seg, and loads the data.

Study area and data. The Buffalo-Cheektowaga metropolitan area: Erie and Niagara counties, New York (about 1.17 million people in 2020). Counts come from the U.S. Census Bureau decennial censuses of 2000, 2010, and 2020, by census tract, for five mutually exclusive groups:

Column Census definition
white Not Hispanic or Latino, White alone
black Not Hispanic or Latino, Black or African American alone
hispanic Hispanic or Latino (any race)
asian Not Hispanic or Latino, Asian alone
other Everyone else (other single races and two or more races)

Tract boundaries change between censuses. To compare years on the same units, the 2000 and 2010 counts have been moved onto the 323 tracts of 2020 with dasymetric interpolation weighted by 2020 housing units; notebook 03_07_07 shows how. Tracts with fewer than 100 residents (water, parks, the airport) are dropped. Data/03_spatial_analysis/data_segregation/make_buffalo_data.py rebuilds everything from the Census files in Data/03_spatial_analysis/data_segregation/.

Two map helpers keep every map in the same frame: add_context(ax) draws the county outlines, and metro_and_core(...) shows the whole metro area next to a 30 km window centred on the City of Buffalo, where most tracts are small.

Installation. This notebook needs installation option 2 (core plus spatial analysis): pip install "geolattice[analysis]". See “Installation options” in the GeoLattice README.

Part B: Moving counts between geographies

B1. Areal interpolation

seg.areal_interpolate(source, target, cols) intersects the two layers and splits each source polygon’s counts across the pieces in proportion to area:

\[\hat{x}_{jk} = \sum_s x_{sk}\,\frac{|s \cap j|}{|s|}.\]

It assumes people are spread uniformly within each source tract, which is badly wrong for a large suburban tract that is half farmland. Counts are conserved exactly.

B2. Dasymetric interpolation

seg.dasymetric_interpolate(..., raster_path=...) replaces area with the sum of an ancillary raster inside each piece:

\[\hat{x}_{jk} = \sum_s x_{sk}\,\frac{R(s \cap j)}{R(s)}.\]

Here the raster is housing_units_2020_50m.tif: the 2020 census count of housing units in each block, spread over 50 m cells. People are placed where homes are.

B3. Does the raster help? A check against block-level truth

The interpolation can be tested where the truth is known. The 2020 census reports population for every block, so we can compute the true 2020 population of any polygon by summing the blocks whose internal point falls inside it. The test mimics the real task:

  1. Sum 2020 block populations into the 2000 (or 2010) tract polygons. This is what a “2020 census on old boundaries” would report.
  2. Interpolate those totals onto the 2020 tracts with each method.
  3. Compare with the true block sums for the 2020 tracts.

The raster comes from 2020 housing units in the same blocks, so the test is favourable to the dasymetric method. It still answers the practical question: does placing people where homes are beat placing them evenly over land? The tracts that matter are those whose boundaries changed; for unchanged tracts both methods are exact.

Code 
# ===================================================================
# B3. Does the raster help? A check against block-level truth.
# With areal weighting, each 2000 tract's population is spread evenly over its land, including
# ===================================================================

blocks = gpd.read_file(DATA / "blocks_2020_points.geojson")  # load vector features from disk

def block_sum(polys):
    j = gpd.sjoin(blocks, polys[["tract_id", "geometry"]], predicate="within", how="inner")  # define j
    return j.groupby("tract_id").pop20.sum().reindex(polys.tract_id).fillna(0).to_numpy()  # return the computed result

truth20 = block_sum(target)  # define truth20
rows, store = [], {}
for src_year in (2000, 2010):  # iterate over the collection

    src["total"] = block_sum(src)  # assign a derived column or cell
    changed = ~target.tract_id.isin(src.tract_id).to_numpy()  # NumPy array view of the column

display(pd.DataFrame(rows).set_index(["from", "method"]).round(1))  # show a rich table preview

On the 2020 tracts whose boundaries changed, dasymetric interpolation has a median error of about 5%, against 11% to 13% for areal weighting, and only 4 tracts are off by more than 25% (against 17 to 29). Large suburban tracts that were split along the edge of a subdivision are exactly where the uniform-density assumption fails. This is why the panel in the setup cell, and in notebooks 03_07_01 to 03_07_06, uses the dasymetric method.

Part D: Why did segregation change?

D1. The Shapley decomposition

Write the index as a function of two ingredients: the spatial pattern \(S\) (the relative distribution of the focal group across tracts) and the composition \(C\) (the focal group’s share of the two-group population). Between periods A and B, \(I(S_B, C_B) - I(S_A, C_A)\) can be attributed to each ingredient by switching it on while holding the other fixed, averaged over both orders (a two-player Shapley value):

\[\Delta_{S} = \tfrac12\big[(I(S_B, C_A) - I(S_A, C_A)) + (I(S_B, C_B) - I(S_A, C_B))\big],\] \[\Delta_{C} = \tfrac12\big[(I(S_A, C_B) - I(S_A, C_A)) + (I(S_B, C_B) - I(S_B, C_A))\big].\]

The counterfactuals \(I(S_A, C_B)\) rescale period A’s focal-group counts in every tract so the metro-wide share matches period B, keeping tract totals. By construction \(\Delta_S + \Delta_C = I_B - I_A\) exactly. The first group in groups is the focal group.

Summary

  • Census tracts change every decade. Indices computed on different geographies are not comparable (MAUP): put every year on common units before comparing.
  • Areal interpolation conserves counts and assumes uniform density; dasymetric interpolation with a housing-unit raster places people where homes are and, checked against block-level truth, is far more accurate for Buffalo’s large suburban and rural tracts.
  • harmonize_panel, compute_panel, and trend turn a multi-year collection into a panel of indices with a trend test in a few lines.
  • The Shapley decomposition splits change exactly into spatial re-sorting and composition shifts; chaining it across decades and across group pairs shows which part drives each trend.
  • export_for_causal produces tract-by-year tables for downstream causal models.
