Multiscale segregation profiles

Overview

Every segregation index answers “how segregated?” at one scale: the scale of the tract or of the chosen neighbourhood. Segregation is not scale-free. Two metro areas with the same tract-level index can differ completely in how far their segregation extends: one may have a few homogeneous blocks scattered through a mixed city, the other a single large divide between city and suburbs.

A multiscale segregation profile (Reardon et al., 2008; Olteanu et al., 2019) follows each tract’s local environment outward. At radius \(r\) it pools the population of every tract within \(r\) and measures how far that pooled composition is from the metro composition. As \(r\) grows the environment eventually becomes the whole metro area and the divergence falls to zero; the speed of that fall describes the scale of segregation.

This notebook follows the PySAL tutorials on multiscalar profiles and the KL divergence profile using seg.compute_profile on Buffalo’s census tracts (four named groups).

Topics:

  • Divergence measures: Kullback-Leibler (KL) and L1
  • Profile curves for individual tracts
  • Mappable summaries: segregation scale, area under the curve, distortion
  • Sensitivity to the threshold and divergence choice

Setup

The next cell finds the repository root, puts it on the import path, points DATA at Data/03_spatial_analysis/data_segregation/buffalo, imports geolattice.spatial_analysis.segregation as seg, and loads the data.

Study area and data. The Buffalo-Cheektowaga metropolitan area: Erie and Niagara counties, New York (about 1.17 million people in 2020). Counts come from the U.S. Census Bureau decennial censuses of 2000, 2010, and 2020, by census tract, for five mutually exclusive groups:

Column Census definition
white Not Hispanic or Latino, White alone
black Not Hispanic or Latino, Black or African American alone
hispanic Hispanic or Latino (any race)
asian Not Hispanic or Latino, Asian alone
other Everyone else (other single races and two or more races)

Tract boundaries change between censuses. To compare years on the same units, the 2000 and 2010 counts have been moved onto the 323 tracts of 2020 with dasymetric interpolation weighted by 2020 housing units; notebook 03_07_07 shows how. Tracts with fewer than 100 residents (water, parks, the airport) are dropped. Data/03_spatial_analysis/data_segregation/make_buffalo_data.py rebuilds everything from the Census files in Data/03_spatial_analysis/data_segregation/.

Two map helpers keep every map in the same frame: add_context(ax) draws the county outlines, and metro_and_core(...) shows the whole metro area next to a 30 km window centred on the City of Buffalo, where most tracts are small.

Installation. This notebook needs installation option 2 (core plus spatial analysis): pip install "geolattice[analysis]". See “Installation options” in the GeoLattice README.

Part A: Profiles for individual tracts

For tract \(i\) and radius \(r\), let \(\tilde{p}_{ik}(r)\) be group \(k\)’s share among everyone living in tracts whose centroid is within \(r\) of tract \(i\) (including \(i\) itself). Two divergences from the metro composition \(P_k\) are available:

\[\text{KL}_i(r) = \sum_k \tilde{p}_{ik}(r)\,\ln\frac{\tilde{p}_{ik}(r)}{P_k}, \qquad \text{L1}_i(r) = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_k |\tilde{p}_{ik}(r) - P_k|.\]

KL is the measure used in Reardon’s profiles and in PySAL’s kl_divergence_profile; it is the same entropy logic as Theil’s \(H\). L1 is bounded in \([0, 1]\) and reads as “the share of the local population that would need to change group for the environment to match the metro area”.

seg.compute_profile evaluates every tract at every radius in one pass. Radius 0 means the tract on its own. Buffalo’s tracts range from a few hundred metres across in the city to more than 10 km in rural Erie County, so the radii run to 30 km.

Reading the curves:

  • The East Side tract has by far the highest divergence at radius 0 and stays high out to 3 km, because its neighbours are also mostly Black. It drops sharply only between 3 and 8 km, once the ring crosses into the surrounding white neighbourhoods. The East Side cluster is about that wide.
  • The West Side tract also starts very high but falls by half within 1 to 2 km: the Hispanic concentration is a much smaller pocket. Its curve then rises again near 5 km as the ring reaches the East Side.
  • The Amherst tract starts moderately high and converges within about 5 km.
  • The outer suburb starts low (0.23) but stays above the metro mean out to 20 km: a low level of difference that extends a long way. The white suburban and rural ring is a very large homogeneous area.
  • The most diverse tract also starts high. Diverse is not the same as typical: in a metro area that is 73% white, a tract with four equal groups is far from the metro mix.

The radius-0 value alone does not predict any of these shapes, which is the information a single-scale index throws away.

Part B: Mappable summaries

A curve per tract is hard to map. MultiscaleProfile stores three summaries, one value per tract:

Summary Definition Reads as
segregation_scale smallest radius where divergence falls below a threshold how far you must go before the environment looks like the metro (inf = beyond the largest radius)
auc area under the divergence-radius curve segregation integrated over all scales
distortion divergence at the largest radius / divergence at radius 0 share of the tract’s segregation that persists at the metro scale
Code

# Part B: Mappable summaries.
# Reading the curves:
# ===================================================================

def capped_scale(pr, cap):
    return np.where(np.isfinite(pr.segregation_scale), pr.segregation_scale, cap) / 1000  # return the computed result

The segregation scale depends on the threshold. There is no universal value; choose one that is small relative to the radius-0 divergences, report it, and check that conclusions hold for neighbouring values.

KL and L1 order tracts similarly at any one radius, but KL falls faster with radius because it weights departures roughly quadratically when they are small. Thresholds are therefore not interchangeable between the two measures. KL is the conventional choice; L1 is easier to explain.

Part D: A metro-wide profile, and a lesson about Theil’s \(H\)

Averaging tract profiles with population weights gives one curve per year. At radius 0 this weighted mean has a precise meaning: it is the mutual information between tract and group, which equals \(H \times E\) (Theil’s \(H\) times the metro entropy). So the profile also shows why \(H\) changed.

Code

# ===================================================================

Which statement is “right” depends on the question: the normalized \(H\) answers “how segregated relative to how diverse”; the mutual information answers “how much does place predict group”. Report both when diversity is changing.

Part F: Scaling to many units

Buffalo’s 323 tracts are small. Block groups or census blocks (about 15,000 populated blocks here) quickly make the problem large. A profile needs, for every unit, the cumulative population within each radius. The direct approach builds an \(n \times n\) distance matrix, which needs \(8n^2\) bytes: 800 MB at \(n = 10{,}000\) and 1.8 GB for Buffalo’s 15,000 populated blocks. compute_profile queries a cKDTree for each unit’s nearest neighbours in chunks, so memory grows with \(n \times\) (neighbours per unit). (Recent PySAL releases also fall back to streaming KD-tree queries when the dense matrix would not fit; older ones did not.)

The benchmark uses the real 2020 census blocks of Erie and Niagara counties (block internal points with population; group counts are not published at block level in this file, so synthetic group labels are drawn from the metro shares, which affects only the values, not the cost). It compares compute_profile with a dense NumPy implementation on random subsets.

Code 
# ===================================================================
# Part F: Scaling to many units.
# Buffalo's 323 tracts are small. Block groups or census blocks (about 15,000 populated blocks here)
# ===================================================================

import time  # library import used below
from scipy.spatial.distance import cdist  # library import used below

blocks = gpd.read_file(DATA / "blocks_2020_points.geojson")  # load vector features from disk
blocks = blocks[blocks.pop20 > 0].reset_index(drop=True)  # index rows for keyed lookup / display
print(f"{len(blocks):,} populated 2020 blocks, {blocks.pop20.sum():,.0f} people")  # status line for the notebook reader

def dense_profile(xy, counts, radii):
    D = cdist(xy, xy)  # define D
    P = counts.sum(0) / counts.sum()  # sum the selected values
    out = np.empty((len(xy), len(radii)))  # define out
    for j, r in enumerate(radii):  # iterate with a numeric index
        pooled = (D <= r).astype(float) @ counts  # tract table with kernel-pooled group counts
        q = pooled / pooled.sum(1, keepdims=True)  # define q
        out[:, j] = np.sum(np.where(q > 0, q * np.log(q / P), 0), axis=1)  # temporary plot column for the choropleth
    return out  # return the computed result

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)  # reproducible random generator
P20 = (tracts[2020][GROUPS].sum() / tracts[2020][GROUPS].sum().sum()).to_numpy()  # sum the selected values
bench, radii_b = [], [250, 500, 1000]
for n in [1000, 2500, 5000, 10000, len(blocks)]:  # iterate over the collection
    sub = blocks.sample(n, random_state=1) if n < len(blocks) else blocks  # define sub
    counts = np.array([rng.multinomial(int(t), P20) for t in sub.pop20]).astype(float)  # define counts
    pts = gpd.GeoDataFrame(pd.DataFrame(counts, columns=GROUPS), geometry=sub.geometry.values, crs=sub.crs)  # build table pts
    t0 = time.perf_counter(); kd = seg.compute_profile(pts, GROUPS, radii=radii_b, chunk_size=1000); t_kd = time.perf_counter() - t0  # define t0
    row = {"units": n, "KD-tree time (s)": t_kd, "dense matrix memory (GB)": 8 * n**2 / 1e9}  # define row
    if n <= 5000:  # conditional branch
        xy = np.c_[pts.geometry.x, pts.geometry.y]  # define xy
        t0 = time.perf_counter(); dn = dense_profile(xy, counts, radii_b); row["dense time (s)"] = time.perf_counter() - t0  # define t0
        row["max abs diff"] = float(np.abs(dn - kd.profile).max())  # absolute gap between group share schedules
    bench.append(row)  # append this year/group record
bench = pd.DataFrame(bench).set_index("units")  # build table bench
bench.round(4)  # run this analysis or plotting step

Both approaches give the same numbers (maximum difference zero). For a few thousand units the dense method is faster, because a matrix product is very efficient. Its memory, however, grows with \(n^2\): 1.8 GB for all 15,076 populated blocks in Erie and Niagara counties, and 200 GB or more for a large metro area. The KD-tree approach handles all Buffalo blocks in about 3 seconds with memory that grows roughly linearly.

Two practical notes:

  • max_neighbors (default 2000) caps how many neighbours each unit can pool. If a unit has more units within the largest radius, compute_profile raises an error rather than silently truncating. Raise it, or shrink the largest radius.
  • Radii are Euclidean distances between centroids in the layer’s CRS, so use a projected CRS in metres (here NAD83 / UTM zone 17N).

Summary

  • A multiscale profile tracks how a tract’s surrounding population converges to the metro composition as the radius grows. Level and reach of segregation are separate properties.
  • segregation_scale, auc, and distortion turn the profiles into maps; the scale depends on a threshold that should be reported and varied.
  • KL and L1 rank tracts alike but decay at different rates, so thresholds are measure-specific.
  • The population-weighted profile at radius 0 is the mutual information \(H \times E\). Comparing it with \(H\) separates a change in sorting from a change in diversity.
  • compute_profile matches PySAL’s KL profile and handles block-level data without an \(n \times n\) matrix.
