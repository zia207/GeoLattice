Local measures of segregation

Overview

Region-wide indices answer “how segregated is the Buffalo metro area?” with one number. Planners, health researchers, and housing agencies usually need the follow-up: where is it segregated, and are those places different from what chance alone would produce? Local measures give one value per tract, ready to map, much like LISA statistics do for spatial autocorrelation.

This notebook follows the PySAL segregation tutorial on local measures using geolattice.spatial_analysis.segregation. Local measures use all five groups (ALL_GROUPS) so that each tract’s shares are computed from its full population.

Topics:

  • Location quotients and exposure deviations for each group
  • Local entropy and Simpson diversity
  • Each tract’s contribution to the dissimilarity index
  • Permutation significance with false-discovery-rate control
  • Spatially pooled (“generalized”) local measures
  • Local change between census years and export to causal models

Setup

The next cell finds the repository root, puts it on the import path, points DATA at Data/03_spatial_analysis/data_segregation/buffalo, imports geolattice.spatial_analysis.segregation as seg, and loads the data.

Study area and data. The Buffalo-Cheektowaga metropolitan area: Erie and Niagara counties, New York (about 1.17 million people in 2020). Counts come from the U.S. Census Bureau decennial censuses of 2000, 2010, and 2020, by census tract, for five mutually exclusive groups:

Column Census definition
white Not Hispanic or Latino, White alone
black Not Hispanic or Latino, Black or African American alone
hispanic Hispanic or Latino (any race)
asian Not Hispanic or Latino, Asian alone
other Everyone else (other single races and two or more races)

Tract boundaries change between censuses. To compare years on the same units, the 2000 and 2010 counts have been moved onto the 323 tracts of 2020 with dasymetric interpolation weighted by 2020 housing units; notebook 03_07_07 shows how. Tracts with fewer than 100 residents (water, parks, the airport) are dropped. Data/03_spatial_analysis/data_segregation/make_buffalo_data.py rebuilds everything from the Census files in Data/03_spatial_analysis/data_segregation/.

Two map helpers keep every map in the same frame: add_context(ax) draws the county outlines, and metro_and_core(...) shows the whole metro area next to a 30 km window centred on the City of Buffalo, where most tracts are small.

Installation. This notebook needs installation option 2 (core plus spatial analysis): pip install "geolattice[analysis]". See “Installation options” in the GeoLattice README.

Part A: Location quotients

The location quotient compares a group’s share in a tract with its share in the region:

\[LQ_{ik} = \frac{p_{ik}}{P_k} = \frac{x_{ik}/t_i}{X_k/T}.\]

\(LQ > 1\): the group is over-represented in the tract; \(LQ < 1\): under-represented; \(LQ = 1\): the tract matches the metro area. Because it is a ratio, the LQ is unbounded above and becomes large for small groups, a point Part B returns to.

Code 
# ===================================================================
# Part A: Location quotients.
# The **location quotient** compares a group's share in a tract with its share in the region:
# ===================================================================

g = tracts[2020]  # working copy of one year's tracts
norm = plt.matplotlib.colors.TwoSlopeNorm(vmin=0, vcenter=1, vmax=6)  # define norm
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(19, 5.4), constrained_layout=True)  # create figure and axes

Each group has its own geography in 2020:

  • White residents are over-represented almost everywhere outside the city core. Because they are 73% of the metro area, their LQ cannot exceed about 1.37, so the white map shows mainly where white residents are absent: the East Side.
  • Black residents are concentrated on the East Side, north-east of City Hall, with LQ up to 6.8, meaning tracts that are more than 80% Black.
  • Hispanic residents are concentrated on the West Side along the Niagara River north of downtown (LQ up to 10), with a second, weaker cluster in South Buffalo.
  • Asian residents form two separate clusters: north of the city around the University at Buffalo’s North Campus in Amherst, and in city neighbourhoods on the East and West Sides. The two clusters most likely reflect different migration streams, university and professional migration in the suburbs and refugee resettlement in the city.

Part B: Exposure deviation and diversity

The exposure deviation \(p_{ik} - P_k\) carries the same information as the LQ on an additive scale. It stays bounded in \([-1, 1]\), so it behaves better for small groups: a tract that is 17% Asian in a metro area that is 4% Asian has \(LQ \approx 4\), but a deviation of only 0.13.

Part C: Where does dissimilarity come from?

Because \(D = \frac{1}{2}\sum_i |x_i/X - y_i/Y|\) is a sum over tracts, each tract’s term is its contribution to the metro-wide index. seg.local_dissimilarity_contribution returns these terms (the focal group is groups[0]), and they add back to \(D\) exactly.

Code

# Part C: Where does dissimilarity come from?.
# Because $D = \frac{1}{2}\sum_i |x_i/X - y_i/Y|$ is a sum over tracts, each tract's term is its
# ===================================================================

pair = ["black", "white"]  # focal two-group pairing for D / Gini
contrib = {y: seg.local_dissimilarity_contribution(gg, pair) for y, gg in tracts.items()}  # define contrib
for y, c in contrib.items():  # iterate key/value pairs
    s_ = np.sort(c)[::-1]  # define s_
    print(f"{y}: sum of contributions = {c.sum():.6f}   D = {seg.dissimilarity(tracts[y], pair):.6f}   "  # two-group dissimilarity index D
          f"{np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(s_) / s_.sum(), 0.5) + 1} tracts make half of D")  # run this analysis or plotting step

Part D: Is a local value more extreme than chance?

Every tract has a finite population, so even in a perfectly integrated metro area some tracts would show \(LQ \neq 1\) by chance. seg.local_significance tests each tract against an evenness null:

  1. Keep each tract’s total \(t_i\).
  2. Redraw its group counts from a multinomial with the metro proportions \(P_k\) (999 times).
  3. Compare the observed statistic with that tract’s simulated distribution (pseudo p-value).
  4. Apply the Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction across all 323 tests.

Step 4 matters. At \(\alpha = 0.05\) about 5% of tracts would be “significant” by chance alone without a correction, the same multiple-testing problem that LISA cluster maps face.

D1. Choose a statistic centred on zero

With alternative="two-sided" the test counts a simulation as “at least as extreme” when \(|\text{null}| \ge |\text{observed}|\). That is correct for a statistic centred on 0, such as the exposure deviation \(p_{ik} - P_k\). The LQ is centred on 1 and always positive, so a two-sided test on the LQ can only ever flag over-representation. The cell shows the consequence.

Code 
# ===================================================================
# Part D: Is a local value more extreme than chance?.
# The largest single contributions come from the East Side core, where each tract's Black share is
# ===================================================================

lq_test = seg.local_significance(g, ALL_GROUPS, seg.local_quotient, group="black", permutations=999, seed=42)  # define lq_test
dev_test = seg.local_significance(g, ALL_GROUPS, seg.local_exposure_deviation, group="black", permutations=999, seed=42)  # define dev_test
pd.DataFrame({  # run this analysis or plotting step
    "LQ, two-sided": {"over-represented (sig.)": int((lq_test.significant_fdr & (lq_test.statistic > 1)).sum()),  # run this analysis or plotting step
                      "under-represented (sig.)": int((lq_test.significant_fdr & (lq_test.statistic < 1)).sum())},  # run this analysis or plotting step
    "deviation, two-sided": {"over-represented (sig.)": int((dev_test.significant_fdr & (dev_test.statistic > 0)).sum()),  # run this analysis or plotting step
                             "under-represented (sig.)": int((dev_test.significant_fdr & (dev_test.statistic < 0)).sum())},  # run this analysis or plotting step
})
LQ, two-sided deviation, two-sided

The LQ test misses every tract where Black residents are significantly under-represented, although most suburban tracts clearly are. The deviation test finds both sides. Two safe options: test the deviation two-sided (used below), or test the LQ twice with alternative="greater" and alternative="less".

Almost every tract is significant in every year, and about two thirds of significant tracts are ones where Black residents are under-represented, mostly the suburban ring. With a median of about 3,400 residents per tract, a random draw almost never strays more than a few percentage points from the metro share, so even moderate real differences are detectable. Only a handful of tracts, mostly in the transition zone around the East Side, are indistinguishable from the metro average. Statistical significance says a tract is not a random draw of the metro population; the magnitude of the deviation says whether the difference matters. Report both.

What the evenness null does and does not test. It asks whether the tract differs from a random draw of residents. It does not ask whether the spatial pattern of tracts is unusual; for that, use the systematic or spatially constrained nulls in notebook 03_07_05.

D3. Significance for every group

Code 
# ===================================================================
# D3. Significance for every group.
# Almost every tract is significant in every year, and about two thirds of significant tracts are
# ===================================================================

rows, sig_all = [], {}
for grp in GROUPS:  # loop over demographic groups
    s = seg.local_significance(g, ALL_GROUPS, seg.local_exposure_deviation, group=grp, permutations=499, seed=7)  # define s

                 "not significant": int((~s.significant_fdr).sum())})  # run this analysis or plotting step

Part E: Spatially pooled local measures

A tract boundary is an administrative line. Residents experience the surrounding area too. Passing a Neighborhood to any local function pools counts from nearby tracts before computing the statistic:

\[\tilde{x}_{ik} = x_{ik} + \sum_j w_{ij}\,x_{jk}\quad\text{(weights row-standardized)}.\]

seg.kernel_weights uses a distance-decay kernel; seg.knn_weights uses the \(k\) nearest tracts.

Code

Part G: Local segregation as a model variable

seg.export_for_causal returns a geometry-free table keyed by tract id: the shape that geolattice.spatial_analysis.spatialcausal and PyCausalML expect for an exposure or confounder column. Stacking the years gives a tract-by-year panel ready to merge with health, school, or housing outcomes by tract_id.

Code

Summary

  • Location quotients and exposure deviations show where each group is over- or under-represented. The LQ is relative and large for small groups; the deviation is bounded.
  • Local entropy summarizes all groups; low values flag single-group tracts, in Buffalo both the suburban ring and the East Side core.
  • Dissimilarity contributions add up to \(D\) exactly, showing which tracts produce the metro-wide index.
  • Permutation tests with FDR correction separate real local concentration from small-number noise. Correct for running one test per tract, use a zero-centred statistic (or two one-sided tests) for two-sided questions, and report magnitudes next to significance.
  • Pooled local measures describe the residential environment rather than the tract, and the choice of neighbourhood changes the map.
  • Local measures on shared boundaries can be differenced across years and exported as a tract-by-year panel for modelling.
