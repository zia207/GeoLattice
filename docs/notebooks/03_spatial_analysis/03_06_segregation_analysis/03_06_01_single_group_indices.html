Single-group segregation indices

Overview

Single-group indices summarize how one group is distributed across spatial units relative to another group (or to everyone else) in a single number. They are the oldest and most cited segregation measures and still anchor most applied work in sociology, public health, and urban economics.

Buffalo is a good place to learn them. It has been one of the most segregated metropolitan areas in the United States for decades, with a Black population concentrated on the city’s East Side, east of Main Street, and a mostly white suburban ring. Its refugee resettlement since the 2000s has also brought fast growth of Asian and Hispanic populations.

Massey and Denton (1988) grouped segregation indices into five dimensions. This notebook covers the two that single-group indices measure most directly:

Dimension Question Indices here
Evenness Are the two groups spread across tracts in the same proportions? Dissimilarity \(D\), Gini \(G\)
Exposure How much contact does a typical member of a group have with others? Isolation \(xPx\), Exposure \(xPy\), Correlation ratio \(\eta^2\)

All functions come from geolattice.spatial_analysis.segregation (seg). The notebook follows the PySAL segregation tutorial (Single-group indices), and Part G checks every value against PySAL itself.

Topics:

  • A worked toy example: computing \(D\) by hand
  • Evenness: dissimilarity, Gini, and the segregation curve
  • Exposure: isolation, exposure (interaction), and the correlation ratio
  • Pairwise contrasts vs. focal group against everyone else
  • Generalized spatial versions and the checkerboard problem
  • Validation against PySAL and the seg.compute() entry point

Setup

The next cell finds the repository root, puts it on the import path, points DATA at Data/03_spatial_analysis/data_segregation/buffalo, imports geolattice.spatial_analysis.segregation as seg, and loads the data.

Study area and data. The Buffalo-Cheektowaga metropolitan area: Erie and Niagara counties, New York (about 1.17 million people in 2020). Counts come from the U.S. Census Bureau decennial censuses of 2000, 2010, and 2020, by census tract, for five mutually exclusive groups:

Column Census definition
white Not Hispanic or Latino, White alone
black Not Hispanic or Latino, Black or African American alone
hispanic Hispanic or Latino (any race)
asian Not Hispanic or Latino, Asian alone
other Everyone else (other single races and two or more races)

Tract boundaries change between censuses. To compare years on the same units, the 2000 and 2010 counts have been moved onto the 323 tracts of 2020 with dasymetric interpolation weighted by 2020 housing units; notebook 03_07_07 shows how. Tracts with fewer than 100 residents (water, parks, the airport) are dropped. Data/03_spatial_analysis/data_segregation/make_buffalo_data.py rebuilds everything from the Census files in Data/03_spatial_analysis/data_segregation/.

Two map helpers keep every map in the same frame: add_context(ax) draws the county outlines, and metro_and_core(...) shows the whole metro area next to a 30 km window centred on the City of Buffalo, where most tracts are small.

Installation. This notebook needs installation option 2 (core plus spatial analysis): pip install "geolattice[analysis]". See “Installation options” in the GeoLattice README.

Two things stand out.

  • Where. Black residents are concentrated in a contiguous block of tracts on Buffalo’s East Side, north-east of City Hall and east of Main Street, with smaller concentrations in Niagara Falls and Lackawanna. A few isolated rural tracts with high shares most likely contain state correctional facilities, whose populations the census counts where they are held. Outside these places, most of the two counties’ tracts have very few Black residents.
  • Change. Between 2000 and 2020 the East Side core lightens at its edges and the Black population spreads north and east into neighbouring city tracts and inner suburbs such as Cheektowaga. Meanwhile the metro area’s composition shifts: the white share falls from 83% to 73%, the Hispanic share doubles, and the Asian share triples, largely through immigration and refugee resettlement. Unlike a fixed-composition example, both where people live and who lives in the region are changing, a distinction that matters again in notebook 03_07_07.

Part B: A toy example

Before computing indices on the metro area, build intuition on a four-tract region where everything can be checked by hand. With \(x_i\) and \(y_i\) the counts of two groups in tract \(i\) and \(X\), \(Y\) their regional totals, the index of dissimilarity (Duncan and Duncan, 1955) is

\[D = \frac{1}{2}\sum_i \left| \frac{x_i}{X} - \frac{y_i}{Y} \right|.\]

It equals the share of either group that would have to move to another tract for every tract to have the regional composition.

Code 
# ===================================================================
# Part B: A toy example.
# Before computing indices on the metro area, build intuition on a four-tract region where everything
# ===================================================================

toy = pd.DataFrame({  # four-tract toy city for a by-hand D
    "tract": ["A", "B", "C", "D"],
    "x": [90, 80, 10, 20],
    "y": [10, 20, 90, 80],
})
X, Y = toy.x.sum(), toy.y.sum()  # run this analysis or plotting step
toy["x/X"] = toy.x / X  # group's share of its regional total
toy["y/Y"] = toy.y / Y  # group's share of its regional total
toy["|diff|"] = (toy["x/X"] - toy["y/Y"]).abs()  # group's share of its regional total
display(toy)  # show a rich table preview
D_hand = 0.5 * toy["|diff|"].sum()  # manual dissimilarity from the |diff| column
print(f"D by hand: {D_hand:.3f}   seg.dissimilarity: {seg.dissimilarity(toy, ['x', 'y']):.3f}")  # two-group dissimilarity index D

even = pd.DataFrame({"x": [50, 50, 50, 50], "y": [50, 50, 50, 50]})  # toy city with identical composition everywhere

print(f"identical composition everywhere: D = {seg.dissimilarity(even, ['x', 'y']):.3f}")  # two-group dissimilarity index D
print(f"no tract shared by both groups:   D = {seg.dissimilarity(apart, ['x', 'y']):.3f}")  # two-group dissimilarity index D

Part C: Evenness

C1. Dissimilarity and Gini

The segregation Gini (James and Taeuber, 1985) uses the same information as \(D\) but averages over every pair of tracts rather than taking a single maximum gap. With \(p_i = x_i / t_i\) the focal share in tract \(i\) (here \(t_i = x_i + y_i\)), and \(P\) the regional share,

\[G = \frac{\sum_i \sum_j t_i t_j \, |p_i - p_j|}{2 T^2 P (1-P)}.\]

seg.gini computes this through the Lorenz-curve trapezoid rule in \(O(n \log n)\) instead of the \(O(n^2)\) double sum. \(G \ge D\) always holds, because \(D\) is the largest vertical gap in the segregation curve while \(G\) is twice the area between that curve and the diagonal.

C2. The segregation curve

Sort tracts by their focal share \(p_i\) and plot the cumulative share of the comparison group (horizontal) against the cumulative share of the focal group (vertical). Perfect evenness is the diagonal. \(D\) is the maximum vertical distance from the diagonal; \(G\) is twice the area between the curve and the diagonal.

Code 
# ===================================================================
# C2. The segregation curve.
# Sort tracts by their focal share $p_i$ and plot the cumulative share of the comparison group
# ===================================================================

def segregation_curve(g, focal, other):
    p = g[focal] / (g[focal] + g[other])  # tract share of the focal group within the pair
    order = np.argsort(p.to_numpy())  # tracts sorted by focal-group share
    cx = np.r_[0, np.cumsum(g[other].to_numpy()[order]) / g[other].sum()]  # cum. share of the other group (segregation curve x)
    cy = np.r_[0, np.cumsum(g[focal].to_numpy()[order]) / g[focal].sum()]  # cum. share of the focal group (segregation curve y)
    return cx, cy  # return the computed result

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.6, 5.4))  # create figure and axes
for y, col in zip(YEARS, ["#3f007d", "#807dba", "#bcbddc"]):  # loop over panel years  # iterate paired sequences together
    cx, cy = segregation_curve(tracts[y], "black", "white")  # run this analysis or plotting step
    ax.plot(cx, cy, color=col, lw=2, label=f"{y}  (D={evenness.loc[y, 'dissimilarity D']:.2f})")  # draw geometries or a series on the axes
    k = np.argmax(np.abs(cx - cy))  # index of maximum vertical gap (links to D)
    ax.plot([cx[k], cx[k]], [cy[k], cx[k]], color=col, ls=":", lw=1.2)  # draw geometries or a series on the axes
ax.plot([0, 1], [0, 1], color="k", lw=0.8)  # draw geometries or a series on the axes

Black-white dissimilarity is 0.77 in 2000 and 0.69 in 2020: in 2020 more than two thirds of Black (or white) residents would still have to move for every tract to match the metro composition. Values above 0.6 are conventionally called “high” (Massey and Denton, 1993), so Buffalo remains highly segregated despite a steady decline of about 0.04 to 0.05 per decade. The curves hug the horizontal axis on the left: a large share of white residents live in tracts with almost no Black residents.

Part D: Exposure

Evenness asks whether proportions match. Exposure asks what a typical person’s neighbourhood looks like, so it depends on group sizes.

  • Isolation (Bell, 1954): the average focal-group share in the tract of a randomly chosen focal-group member, \(xPx = \sum_i \frac{x_i}{X}\frac{x_i}{t_i}\).
  • Exposure / interaction: the average share of group \(y\) in that tract, \(xPy = \sum_i \frac{x_i}{X}\frac{y_i}{t_i}\). It is asymmetric: \(xPy \ne yPx\) unless both groups are the same size.
  • Correlation ratio (Bell, 1954; White, 1986): isolation rescaled by the regional share \(P\), \(\eta^2 = \frac{xPx - P}{1 - P}\). Isolation can never drop below \(P\) (that is its value under perfect evenness), so \(\eta^2\) removes the group-size effect and runs from 0 to 1.

Here groups=ALL_GROUPS, so \(t_i\) is the full tract population.

Part F: Spatial (generalized) single-group indices

F1. The checkerboard problem

Aspatial indices treat tracts as unrelated bins. White (1983) showed the consequence with a checkerboard: alternating all-Black and all-white squares and two solid halves give the same \(D = 1\), although a resident of the checkerboard lives one block from the other group.

The test below keeps every tract’s composition but shuffles which tract gets which composition (the “systematic” null used in notebook 03_07_05). Aspatial \(D\) does not change at all.

Code

F2. Generalized spatial dissimilarity

The fix (Reardon and O’Sullivan, 2004) is to measure each tract’s composition over its local environment rather than its own boundary. A kernel neighbourhood pools counts from nearby tracts, weighted by distance, and the index is computed on those pooled counts:

\[\tilde{x}_i = x_i + \sum_j w_{ij}\, x_j, \qquad \tilde{D} = D(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}).\]

seg.kernel_weights builds the weights from tract centroids (bandwidth in metres) and Neighborhood.pooled_counts does the pooling. The small wrapper below gives a spatial version of any index in the package.

Code 
# ===================================================================
# F2. Generalized spatial dissimilarity.
# The fix (Reardon and O'Sullivan, 2004) is to measure each tract's composition over its
# ===================================================================

def spatial_index(g, groups, index_fn, bandwidth, kernel="gaussian", **kw):  # aspatial index on kernel-pooled counts
    nb = seg.kernel_weights(g, bandwidth=bandwidth, kernel=kernel)  # distance-decay neighbourhood weights
    pooled = g.copy()  # tract table with kernel-pooled group counts
    pooled[groups] = nb.pooled_counts(g[groups].to_numpy(dtype=float))  # assign a derived column or cell
    return index_fn(pooled, groups, **kw)  # return the computed result

for gg, name in [(g20, "observed"), (shuffled, "shuffled")]:  # iterate over the collection
    print(f"{name:9s} aspatial D = {seg.dissimilarity(gg, pair):.3f}   "  # two-group dissimilarity index D
          f"spatial D (2 km Gaussian) = {spatial_index(gg, pair, seg.dissimilarity, 2000):.3f}")  # aspatial index on kernel-pooled counts

The observed spatial index is only a little below the aspatial one, because Buffalo’s Black neighbourhoods form one large contiguous area: pooling each tract with its neighbours mostly pools similar tracts. That is what “spatially segregated” means, and the aspatial index cannot see it.

Summary

  • Evenness (\(D\), \(G\)) compares the distribution of two groups across units. \(D\) is the share that would need to move; \(G\) averages over all pairs of units and is always at least \(D\).
  • Exposure (isolation, interaction, correlation ratio) describes the typical neighbourhood and depends on group size. Use \(\eta^2\) when comparing groups of different sizes.
  • The comparison group changes the answer. Report pairwise contrasts as well as “group vs. everyone else”.
  • Aspatial indices are blind to the arrangement of units (the checkerboard problem). Pooling counts over a kernel neighbourhood gives a generalized spatial index; check its sensitivity to bandwidth.
  • seg results match PySAL to floating-point precision, and seg.compute(..., ci=True) adds spatially aware uncertainty.
