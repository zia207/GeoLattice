Downscaling Soil Moisture: Six Methods Compared

SMAP measures soil moisture accurately, but at 9 km (from 36 km footprints); farms, catchments and irrigation decisions need 1 km or finer. Downscaling borrows the spatial detail of a finer variable that responds to soil moisture: land surface temperature, an optical index, radar backscatter, soil texture or terrain. Dozens of methods exist, and published comparisons rarely use the same data. This notebook downscales SMAP L3 Enhanced from 9 km to the 1 km EASE grid over central Iowa for the growing seasons of 2019 to 2021 with six families of methods on identical inputs, and tests them in the same way: against the operational SMAP/Sentinel-1 1 km product, against two in situ stations, for mass conservation, and, most importantly, against the trivial baseline of copying each 9 km value into its 81 sub-cells.

Overview

Two rules for any downscaling method

  1. Mass conservation: the 1 km values inside a 9 km cell should average to the 9 km value. Then the method only adds spatial pattern and keeps the level and accuracy of the coarse product.
  2. Beat the baseline: a 1 km map is only useful if it is closer to the truth than the coarse value copied into every sub-cell. Correlating a downscaled map with stations mostly rewards the coarse signal it inherits; what counts is the part it adds.

The six methods

# Method Fine-scale information Idea
0 Baseline none copy each 9 km value to its 81 cells of 1 km
1 Regression + smooth interpolation soil texture, organic carbon, elevation, slope pooled linear regression of coarse anomalies on aggregated static covariates, applied at 1 km as a zero-mean pattern, added to a mass-preserving (pycnophylactic) smooth surface through the 9 km values
2a DisPATCh MODIS LST and NDVI soil evaporative efficiency (SEE) from the LST-NDVI space; SM = SM_c + SMp (SEE - SEE_c) (Merlin et al. 2012)
2b OPTRAM MODIS NBAR SWIR and NDVI the same linear scheme with the OPTRAM moisture index (Sadeghi et al. 2017) instead of SEE
3 Active-passive (SAR) Sentinel-1 VV at 50 m SM = SM_c + beta (sigma - sigma_c) (Das et al. 2014), aggregated to 1 km
4 Machine learning + residual NDVI, rain, texture, terrain, crops, season XGBoost trained on coarse cells, applied at 1 km, coarse residual added back
5 Deep learning (U-Net) the same covariates a residual U-Net that learns the 1 km correction from SPL2SMAP_S 1 km targets
6 Fusion methods 1 to 4 weighted mean with weights from each method’s error in the training years

How the methods are judged

The truth at 1 km is unknown. Two imperfect references are used: the SMAP/Sentinel-1 product SPL2SMAP_S at 1 km (an independent algorithm, but sharing SMAP and Sentinel-1 with methods 3 and 5) and the 5 cm sensors of two stations (true points, but not representative of 1 km). For every method:

  • Within-cell skill: the correlation between the pattern a method adds (method - baseline) and the pattern of the reference (reference - baseline), over all cells and days. Zero means no useful sub-cell information.
  • Gain over the baseline: 1 - ubRMSD(method) / ubRMSD(baseline) against the reference. Positive means better than copying the coarse value.
  • Mass conservation error: the mean absolute difference between the 9 km block mean of the 1 km map and the 9 km value.
  • Availability: the share of cells and days with a value (thermal and SAR methods are gappy).

U-Net and fusion learn from SPL2SMAP_S in 2019 and 2020, so all comparisons with SPL2SMAP_S use 2021 only.

Learning objectives

  1. Build a consistent set of fine-scale predictors on a grid nested in the coarse product.
  2. Implement six families of downscaling methods on identical inputs.
  3. Measure within-cell skill, gain over the coarse baseline, mass conservation and availability.
  4. Decide which method suits which purpose (daily maps, field patterns, clear-sky only, all weather).

Method 1: regression with smooth interpolation

The daily mean is removed from the coarse values; the anomalies are regressed (pooled over all days) on the static covariates averaged to 9 km; the regression is applied at 1 km as a pattern with zero mean in every 9 km cell. It is added to a smooth surface through the coarse values made with the pycnophylactic interpolation (Tobler 1979), which removes the blocky cell edges while keeping every block mean, a simple stand-in for area-to-point kriging.

Code 
t0 = time.time()  # start
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression  # pooled regression
STATIC = ["sand", "clay", "soc", "elevation", "slope"]  # static covariates
Xf = xr.Dataset({v: ds[v] for v in STATIC})  # at 1 km
Xc = xr.Dataset({v: sm.block_mean(ds[v].rio.write_crs("EPSG:6933"), F) for v in STATIC})  # at 9 km
anom = coarse - coarse.mean(("y", "x"))  # daily spatial anomalies at 9 km
tab = anom.to_dataframe(name="a").reset_index()[["time", "y", "x", "a"]].merge(Xc.to_dataframe().reset_index()[["y", "x"] + STATIC], on=["y", "x"]).dropna()  # samples
mu, sdv = tab[STATIC].mean(), tab[STATIC].std()  # standardisation
reg = LinearRegression().fit((tab[STATIC] - mu) / sdv, tab["a"])  # pooled fit
print("standardised coefficients (m3/m3 per std):", dict(zip(STATIC, np.round(reg.coef_, 4))), "| R2", round(reg.score((tab[STATIC] - mu) / sdv, tab["a"]), 2))  # fit
pattern = sum(((Xf[v] - mu[v]) / sdv[v]) * c for v, c in zip(STATIC, reg.coef_))  # static pattern at 1 km
pattern = pattern - sm.nested_upsample(sm.block_mean(pattern.rio.write_crs("EPSG:6933"), F), F).values  # zero block mean: adds pattern, not level
smooth = sm.pycnophylactic(coarse, F, iterations=30)  # smooth surface through the 9 km cells, block means kept
m1 = smooth + pattern.values  # smooth coarse field plus the static pattern
products["1 regression + smooth"] = m1.clip(0.02, 0.6)  # store
timing["1 regression + smooth"] = time.time() - t0  # run time
standardised coefficients (m3/m3 per std): {'sand': np.float32(-0.0096), 'clay': np.float32(-0.0026), 'soc': np.float32(-0.0011), 'elevation': np.float32(-0.0049), 'slope': np.float32(0.0005)} | R2 0.2

The static covariates explain about 20 % of the spatial anomalies between the 72 cells of 9 km; sand has the largest (negative) effect, as sandier soils drain and dry faster, followed by elevation.

Because the pattern has a zero mean in every 9 km block and the smooth surface keeps the block means, method 1 conserves mass by construction. Its sub-cell pattern is the same on every day: a smooth gradient between neighbouring cells plus the texture and terrain pattern of the regression.

Method 2: DisPATCh and OPTRAM

DisPATCh needs a clear-sky day: the LST-NDVI edges define the soil temperature end-members and the soil evaporative efficiency (SEE) of each 1 km cell; SMAP is then redistributed with SM = SM_c + SMp (SEE - SEE_c), where the soil moisture parameter SMp = SM_c / SEE_c is computed per 9 km cell. A domain of 81 x 72 cells is small for drawing edges, so they are fitted on the clear days within eight days of each date, and SMp is capped at 0.45 m3 m-3; both settings were chosen on 2019 and 2020 only (a first version with daily edges and a cap of 0.6 had more than twice the error). OPTRAM uses the SWIR transformed reflectance instead of temperature, with edges fitted once over the three seasons on vegetated pixels (NDVI 0.2 to 0.85).

Code 
t0 = time.time()  # start
dis = xr.full_like(base, np.nan)  # DisPATCh result
used = 0  # clear days processed
WIN, SMP_MAX = 8, 0.45  # edges from the clear days within +-8 days; cap of the soil moisture parameter (chosen on 2019 and 2020)
for i, d in enumerate(days):  # each day
    lst, nd = ds.lst_day.isel(time=i), ds.ndvi.isel(time=i)  # inputs of the day
    c = coarse.isel(time=i)  # SMAP of the day
    if float(lst.notnull().mean()) < 0.5 or float(c.notnull().mean()) < 0.5:  # need a mostly clear day and SMAP
        continue
    sl = slice(max(0, i - WIN), i + WIN + 1)  # neighbouring days
    try:
        e = sm.ts_vi_edges(ds.lst_day.isel(time=sl), ds.ndvi.isel(time=sl), ndvi_bins=20, quantile=0.02, ndvi_range=(0.15, 0.9))  # pooled edges
    except ValueError:  # too few populated NDVI bins
        continue
    see = sm.soil_evaporative_efficiency(lst, nd, edges=e).rio.write_crs("EPSG:6933")  # SEE at 1 km
    smp = (c / sm.block_mean(see, F).values).clip(0.1, SMP_MAX).rio.write_crs("EPSG:6933")  # soil moisture parameter per 9 km cell
    dis[i] = sm.dispatch(c, see, sm_p=smp).values  # DisPATCh
    used += 1  # count
products["2a DisPATCh (LST)"] = dis  # store
timing["2a DisPATCh (LST)"] = time.time() - t0  # run time
print(f"DisPATCh run on {used} of {len(days)} days (clear sky and SMAP)")  # coverage
t0 = time.time()  # start
ndvi_nbar = (ds.nir - ds.red) / (ds.nir + ds.red)  # NDVI from the same NBAR reflectance
strf = sm.str_transform(ds.swir22)  # SWIR transformed reflectance
veg = ndvi_nbar > 0.1  # leave water and bare urban surfaces out of the edge fit

Method 4: machine learning with residual correction

XGBoost learns SMAP 9 km from the covariates averaged to 9 km (cells and days stacked), with spatial block cross-validation, then predicts at 1 km; the coarse residual of each cell is added back. Only gap-free covariates are used, so the result is daily and complete.

Code 
t0 = time.time()  # start

Method 5: a residual U-Net

The network sees the SMAP field interpolated to 1 km and the covariates, and learns the correction to the SPL2SMAP_S 1 km map on the Sentinel-1 days of 2019 and 2020; it is then applied to every day of 2021.

Code

Method 6: fusion

Each of methods 1 to 4 gets a weight from its unbiased error against SPL2SMAP_S in 2019 and 2020 (w = 1 / ubRMSD^2, normalised); where a method has no value (clouds, no Sentinel-1 pass) the weights of the others are renormalised.

Code 
t0 = time.time()  # start
def ubrmsd(a, b):  # unbiased RMS difference over all cells and days
    d = (a - b).values.ravel(); d = d[np.isfinite(d)]  # differences
    return float(np.sqrt(np.mean((d - d.mean()) ** 2))) if d.size > 50 else np.nan  # value
members = ["1 regression + smooth", "2a DisPATCh (LST)", "2b OPTRAM", "3 SAR active-passive", "4 XGBoost + residual"]  # fusion members
train = days.year < 2021  # training years
err = {k: ubrmsd(products[k].sel(time=train), spl.sel(time=train)) for k in members}  # errors in 2019 and 2020
print({k: round(v, 4) for k, v in err.items()})  # errors
fused = sm.merge_products({k: products[k] for k in members}, errors=err, rescale=False)  # weighted mean, no rescaling
products["6 fusion"] = fused.sm  # store
timing["6 fusion"] = time.time() - t0  # run time

The comparison is sobering, and typical of honest downscaling tests.

  • No method beats the baseline against SPL2SMAP_S in 2021. Regression with smoothing (-0.8 %) and XGBoost (-2.5 %) come closest; the thermal and optical methods are 28 to 30 % worse, because their strong contrasts are not matched by the reference.
  • Within-cell skill is small everywhere. The U-Net (0.17) and the SAR method (0.11) agree best with the reference’s sub-cell pattern, but both share information with it: the U-Net was trained on SPL2SMAP_S and the SAR method uses the same Sentinel-1 backscatter that SPL2SMAP_S uses. The methods that use only independent information (texture, terrain, LST, OPTRAM) show no correlation with it.
  • At the stations regression with smoothing is the only method slightly better than the baseline (R 0.48 against 0.47); fusion matches the baseline; the others are worse.
  • Mass conservation holds for every method except the U-Net (0.016 m3 m-3), which learns its own level, and the SAR method, whose values are clipped to a physical range.
  • Availability separates the methods most: regression, XGBoost, U-Net and fusion give a value wherever SMAP has one, OPTRAM on half of the days, DisPATCh on 18 % and the SAR method on 6 %.

Three reasons stand out. Central Iowa is flat and almost entirely corn and soybean, so the true variation of soil moisture inside a 9 km cell is small compared with retrieval noise. The 1 km reference is itself a downscaled product with errors of several hundredths of m3 m-3, so “truth” at 1 km is uncertain. And two stations cannot judge 1 km patterns. A landscape with stronger contrasts (irrigation, soils, terrain) and a dense field network such as SMAPVEX16 would test the methods more sharply.

Summary and exercises

  • On a grid nested in the coarse product, block means and copies are exact, which makes mass conservation and the baseline comparison unambiguous.
  • Six method families were run on identical inputs over central Iowa (April to October 2019 to 2021) and judged on 2021, a year none of them was tuned or trained on against the reference.
  • None beat the baseline of copying the 9 km value against SPL2SMAP_S; regression with a smooth, mass-preserving interpolation came closest and was the only method slightly better at the stations.
  • Methods with some within-cell agreement (U-Net 0.17, SAR 0.11) share information with the reference; methods using only independent covariates showed none.
  • Daily, complete products come from regression, machine learning, U-Net and fusion; DisPATCh is limited to clear days (18 %) and the SAR method to Sentinel-1 days (6 %).
  • A downscaled map is a hypothesis about sub-cell variability: test it against the baseline and against a reference that does not share its inputs before using it.

Exercises

  1. Run method 4 with residual=False. How much do the mass conservation error and the station metrics change?
  2. Fit the DisPATCh edges over a whole month instead of each day. Does the number of usable days or the within-cell skill change?
  3. Add Sentinel-1 VV aggregated to 1 km as a covariate of the XGBoost model on Sentinel-1 days only.
