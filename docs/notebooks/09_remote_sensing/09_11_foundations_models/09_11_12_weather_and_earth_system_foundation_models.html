Weather and Earth System Foundation Models

The largest foundation models in Earth science do not read satellite images at all: they read the gridded state of the atmosphere and predict how it evolves. Since 2022 learned weather models have matched or beaten the best physics-based forecasts at a fraction of the computing cost. This notebook compares the main models in the catalog, then runs Microsoft’s Aurora on real ERA5 reanalysis streamed from WeatherBench2, makes 6 and 12 hour global forecasts and scores them against ERA5 and against a persistence baseline, with a closer look at the US Corn Belt used elsewhere in this chapter.

Overview

From numerical weather prediction to learned emulators

Numerical weather prediction (NWP) solves the equations of the atmosphere on a grid, step by step, on supercomputers. A learned emulator is trained on decades of reanalysis (ERA5: the best estimate of the past atmosphere, hourly at 0.25 degrees since 1940) to map the state at time t to the state at t + 6 h. Applied repeatedly (an autoregressive rollout) it produces a forecast in seconds on one GPU.

Model Provider Architecture Notable for
FourCastNet NVIDIA, 2022 Fourier neural operator (v1), spherical FNO (v2) first competitive learned model; very fast ensembles
GraphCast Google DeepMind, 2023 graph neural network on an icosahedral mesh beat ECMWF HRES on most targets at 10 days
GenCast Google DeepMind, 2024 diffusion model on a mesh probabilistic ensembles that beat ECMWF ENS
Aurora Microsoft, 2024 3-D Swin U-Net with Perceiver encoders, 1.3 B parameters one foundation model fine-tuned for weather, air pollution, ocean waves
Prithvi WxC NASA and IBM, 2024 2.3 B transformer on MERRA-2 downscaling, gravity waves, fine-tuning tasks

Aurora is a foundation model in the full sense of this chapter: pretrained on more than a million hours of diverse atmospheric data, then fine-tuned for tasks.

What these models predict

Aurora reads four surface variables (2 m temperature, 10 m wind components, mean sea level pressure), five atmospheric variables (geopotential, wind components, temperature, specific humidity) on 13 pressure levels, three static variables (surface geopotential, land-sea mask, soil type) and the two most recent states, 6 hours apart, so it sees the tendency.

How forecasts are scored

The root mean square error (RMSE) against the verifying analysis, weighted by the cosine of latitude so that the crowded grid cells near the poles do not dominate, is the standard score. Persistence (tomorrow equals today) is the simplest baseline; any forecast must beat it.

Links to land applications

Weather drives everything else in this chapter: crop calendars (notebook 09_11_06), drought, evapotranspiration (chapter 08_12) and yield. Learned forecasts are fast enough to run large ensembles for those applications.

WeatherBench2 publishes ERA5 as a Zarr store on Google Cloud Storage. Opening it reads only the metadata; values are fetched when needed. The forecast starts from the states at 00 and 06 UTC on 1 January 2023 and is verified at 12 and 18 UTC.

Building an Aurora batch

An Aurora Batch holds tensors of shape (batch, history, …) for surface and atmospheric variables, 2-D static fields, and metadata: latitudes (decreasing), longitudes (0 to 360), the time of the latest state and the pressure levels. The history is the 00 and 06 UTC states.

Code 
from aurora import Batch, Metadata  # Aurora data structures

def make_batch(i0):  # the states at times[i0] and times[i0 + 1] as an Aurora batch
    sl = slice(i0, i0 + 2)  # two consecutive states
    return Batch(
        surf_vars={k: torch.from_numpy(state[v].isel(time=sl).values[None]) for k, v in SURF.items()},  # (1, 2, lat, lon)
        static_vars={k: torch.from_numpy(static[v].values) for k, v in STATIC.items()},  # (lat, lon)
        atmos_vars={k: torch.from_numpy(state[v].isel(time=sl).values[None]) for k, v in ATMOS.items()},  # (1, 2, level, lat, lon)
        metadata=Metadata(lat=torch.from_numpy(era.latitude.values), lon=torch.from_numpy(era.longitude.values),
                          time=(times[i0 + 1].to_pydatetime(),), atmos_levels=tuple(int(l) for l in era.level.values)))  # metadata

batch = make_batch(0)  # history 00 and 06 UTC
print({k: tuple(v.shape) for k, v in batch.surf_vars.items()}, "|", {k: tuple(v.shape) for k, v in batch.atmos_vars.items()})  # shapes
{'2t': (1, 2, 721, 1440), '10u': (1, 2, 721, 1440), '10v': (1, 2, 721, 1440), 'msl': (1, 2, 721, 1440)} | {'z': (1, 2, 13, 721, 1440), 'u': (1, 2, 13, 721, 1440), 'v': (1, 2, 13, 721, 1440), 't': (1, 2, 13, 721, 1440), 'q': (1, 2, 13, 721, 1440)}

A 6 hour forecast

The full Aurora (1.3 billion parameters, pretrained on ERA5 and other sources) needs more memory than a 16 GB GPU offers at 0.25 degrees: its weights alone take 4.8 GB, and a global forecast step on this machine’s GPU ran out of memory at about 13 GB of activations. It runs on the CPU instead, about six minutes per step with 24 cores. On a GPU with 24 GB or more the same code runs on the GPU in seconds. rollout runs two 6 hour steps at once; the first is the +6 h forecast scored here, the second the +12 h forecast scored further down.

Code 
from aurora import AuroraPretrained, rollout  # model and autoregressive rollout

big_gpu = DEVICE == "cuda" and torch.cuda.get_device_properties(0).total_memory >= 24 * 2**30  # enough GPU memory?
AURORA_DEVICE = "cuda" if big_gpu else "cpu"  # where the full model runs
torch.set_num_threads(os.cpu_count())  # use every core on the CPU
model = AuroraPretrained(autocast=big_gpu)  # 1.3 B parameters (bfloat16 autocast on the GPU)
model.load_checkpoint("microsoft/aurora", "aurora-0.25-pretrained.ckpt")  # pretrained weights (about 5 GB, cached)
model = model.to(AURORA_DEVICE).eval()  # inference mode
t0 = time.perf_counter()  # start the clock
with torch.inference_mode():  # inference only
    preds = [p.to("cpu") for p in rollout(model, batch, steps=2)]  # +6 h and +12 h
pred = preds[0]  # the +6 h forecast
print(f"Aurora 0.25 pretrained on {AURORA_DEVICE}: {sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters()) / 1e9:.2f} B parameters | "
      f"two steps in {time.perf_counter() - t0:.0f} s | first forecast valid at {pred.metadata.time[0]}")  # what ran
del model  # free memory
MODEL = "Aurora 0.25 pretrained (1.3 B)"  # label for the figures
Aurora 0.25 pretrained on cpu: 1.26 B parameters | two steps in 873 s | first forecast valid at 2023-01-01 12:00:00

Scoring against ERA5 and persistence

The forecast valid at 12 UTC is compared with the ERA5 analysis at 12 UTC, and so is persistence (the 06 UTC state). Aurora returns a grid with 720 latitudes (it drops the south pole row), so ERA5 is cut to match.

Code 
lat = pred.metadata.lat.cpu().numpy()  # forecast latitudes
w = np.cos(np.deg2rad(lat))[:, None]; w = w / w.mean()  # latitude weights
rmse = lambda a, b: float(np.sqrt(np.nanmean(w * (a - b) ** 2)))  # latitude-weighted RMSE
truth = state.sel(latitude=lat, method="nearest")  # ERA5 on the forecast grid
rows = []  # scores
for k, v in SURF.items():  # surface variables
    f = pred.surf_vars[k][0, 0].float().cpu().numpy()  # forecast
    rows.append({"variable": k, "Aurora +6 h": rmse(f, truth[v].isel(time=2).values), "persistence": rmse(truth[v].isel(time=1).values, truth[v].isel(time=2).values)})  # scores
for k, v, lev in [("z", "geopotential", 500), ("t", "temperature", 850), ("u", "u_component_of_wind", 250)]:  # upper air
    li = list(era.level.values).index(lev)  # level index
    f = pred.atmos_vars[k][0, 0, li].float().cpu().numpy()  # forecast
    rows.append({"variable": f"{k}{lev}", "Aurora +6 h": rmse(f, truth[v].isel(time=2, level=li).values),
                 "persistence": rmse(truth[v].isel(time=1, level=li).values, truth[v].isel(time=2, level=li).values)})  # scores
sc = pd.DataFrame(rows).set_index("variable"); sc["skill vs persistence"] = 1 - sc["Aurora +6 h"] / sc["persistence"]  # skill score
sc.round(3)

Six hours ahead, Aurora’s errors are a small fraction of persistence’s for every variable: 0.49 K against 2.8 K for 2 m temperature, 32 Pa against 265 Pa for sea level pressure, 24 m2 s-2 against 223 for 500 hPa geopotential. The skill score (1 minus the ratio of the two errors) ranges from about 0.71 for 850 hPa temperature to 0.89 for 500 hPa geopotential. Persistence is a weak baseline at 6 hours, because the daily cycle alone moves surface temperature by several kelvin; the point is that the learned model captures that cycle and the moving weather systems from two input states.

The persistence error map is dominated by the daily cycle: between 06 and 12 UTC the sun rises over Africa and Europe (cold errors there, since persistence keeps the night) and sets over East Asia and Australia (warm errors). Aurora’s error map has none of that pattern. What remains is small-scale noise over land, largest over mountains, deserts and the high latitudes of the northern winter, where surface temperature varies over short distances, and a near-zero error over the oceans.

A 12 hour rollout and the Corn Belt

aurora.rollout fed the first forecast back as input, so its second step is a forecast valid at 18 UTC, 12 hours after the last input. The score is computed for the globe and for a box over Iowa (40 to 45 N, 97 to 90 W), the Corn Belt study area of this chapter.

Code 
box = (lat >= 40) & (lat <= 45)  # Iowa latitudes
lon = pred.metadata.lon.cpu().numpy(); lbox = (lon >= 263) & (lon <= 270)  # 97 to 90 W in 0..360
rows = []  # rollout scores
for step, p in enumerate(preds, 1):  # each lead time
    f = p.surf_vars["2t"][0, 0].float().numpy()  # forecast
    tgt = truth["2m_temperature"].isel(time=1 + step).values  # ERA5 at the valid time
    per = truth["2m_temperature"].isel(time=1).values  # persistence from 06 UTC
    rows.append({"lead (h)": 6 * step, "Aurora global": rmse(f, tgt), "persistence global": rmse(per, tgt),
                 "Aurora Iowa": float(np.sqrt(np.mean((f - tgt)[np.ix_(box, lbox)] ** 2))),
                 "persistence Iowa": float(np.sqrt(np.mean((per - tgt)[np.ix_(box, lbox)] ** 2)))})  # scores

Errors grow with lead time, from 0.49 K to 0.61 K globally between 6 and 12 hours, still a fifth of persistence’s error. Over Iowa the errors are larger (0.8 K at 6 hours, 1.4 K at 12 hours): a small, continental, winter region with snow and strong night-time cooling is harder than the global average, which is dominated by oceans. A score over the region of interest, not only the global one, is what an application needs.

Summary and exercises

Summary

  • Learned weather models (FourCastNet, GraphCast, GenCast, Aurora, Prithvi WxC) emulate the atmosphere from reanalysis and forecast in seconds on one GPU.
  • ERA5 can be read directly from the WeatherBench2 Zarr store, field by field, without downloading the archive.
  • Aurora takes surface, atmospheric and static variables with their metadata as a Batch, and rollout produces forecasts at longer lead times.
  • Forecasts are scored with latitude-weighted RMSE against the verifying analysis, and must beat persistence; the skill score shows by how much.

Exercises

  1. Extend the rollout to 4 steps (24 hours) and plot RMSE against lead time for Aurora and persistence.
  2. Score the 500 hPa geopotential (z500) of the rollout, the classic medium-range target.
  3. Start the forecast on 1 July 2022 instead. Is the error over Iowa in summer larger or smaller?
  4. Read the Aurora air-pollution or wave model cards in its documentation and list the extra inputs each needs.
  5. Compute daily maximum 2 m temperature over Iowa from a four-step rollout, the quantity a crop model would use.
