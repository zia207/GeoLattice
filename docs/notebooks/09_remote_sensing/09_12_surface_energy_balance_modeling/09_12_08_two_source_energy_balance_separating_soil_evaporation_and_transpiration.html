Two-Source Energy Balance: Separating Soil Evaporation and Transpiration

A Landsat thermal pixel over an orchard or a young row crop sees a mix of sunlit canopy and hot, often dry, soil. One-source models such as SEBAL and METRIC treat that mix as one surface with one temperature and one resistance, and absorb the difference between the mix and the canopy into the anchor calibration. The two-source energy balance (TSEB) of Norman et al. (1995) instead splits the pixel into a soil and a canopy component, each with its own temperature, net radiation and fluxes. That makes it suitable for partial canopies, and it separates evaporation from the soil from transpiration by the plants, which matters for water productivity and irrigation efficiency. This notebook runs TSEB with the Priestley-Taylor start (TSEB-PT) on the 5 July 2024 scene, maps soil and canopy temperatures and the transpiration fraction, compares it with METRIC, and shows its main sensitivity, the air temperature.

Overview

Splitting the radiometric temperature

The thermal sensor measures one radiometric temperature Trad. TSEB writes it as an emission- weighted mix of canopy temperature Tc and soil temperature Ts:

Trad^4 = f_theta Tc^4 + (1 - f_theta) Ts^4, with f_theta = 1 - exp(-0.5 Omega LAI / cos(theta_v))

f_theta is the fraction of the view filled by vegetation at view angle theta_v (Landsat looks almost straight down) and Omega a clumping factor (1 for a random canopy, below 1 for rows and orchards).

Splitting net radiation

Net radiation is shared between canopy and soil with an extinction law, Rn_s = Rn exp(-kappa Omega LAI / sqrt(2 cos(theta_s))), Rn_c = Rn - Rn_s, with kappa about 0.45 and theta_s the sun zenith angle. Soil heat flux is a fraction of the soil’s net radiation, G = 0.35 Rn_s.

The Priestley-Taylor start

The canopy is first assumed to transpire at the Priestley-Taylor rate, LE_c = alpha_PT fg Delta / (Delta + gamma) Rn_c with alpha_PT = 1.26 (unstressed). Then:

  1. H_c = Rn_c - LE_c, and Tc follows from H_c = rho cp (Tc - Ta) / rah.
  2. Ts follows from the radiometric split.
  3. H_s = rho cp (Ts - Ta) / (rah + rs), with the soil resistance rs of Kustas and Norman (1999), which depends on the wind near the soil and the temperature difference between soil and canopy.
  4. LE_s = Rn_s - G - H_s.

If LE_s comes out negative, which is impossible during the day, the canopy is stressed: alpha_PT is lowered step by step until LE_s is zero or positive. If it reaches zero, the canopy is not transpiring at all.

Parallel and series networks

In the parallel network (used here) soil and canopy exchange heat with the air above the canopy independently. The series network lets both exchange heat with the air inside the canopy space, which then exchanges with the air above; it is more realistic for dense canopies and needs more resistances. pyTSEB (Nieto et al.) implements both, together with many refinements.

ALEXI and DisALEXI

TSEB needs the air temperature Ta, and it is sensitive to it: an error of 1 K in Ta changes H over a hot surface by tens of W m-2. ALEXI (Anderson et al. 1997) removes that dependence by using the rise in surface temperature between early morning and midday from geostationary satellites, coupled to a model of the growing atmospheric boundary layer. DisALEXI then disaggregates the coarse ALEXI fluxes to Landsat resolution by adjusting the air temperature until the Landsat TSEB fluxes match the ALEXI ones; this is the OpenET member of the family.

Learning objectives

  1. Explain how TSEB splits radiometric temperature and net radiation between soil and canopy.
  2. Describe the Priestley-Taylor start and how the model reduces alpha_PT under stress.
  3. Run TSEB-PT on a Landsat scene and map soil and canopy temperatures, soil evaporation and transpiration.
  4. Relate the transpiration fraction to vegetation cover and crop type.
  5. Compare TSEB with METRIC and measure TSEB’s sensitivity to air temperature.

Data

The 5 July 2024 Landsat 8 scene (sd.load_scene), its net radiation and LAI (sd.energy_inputs), the ERA5 air temperature, humidity and wind at the overpass, and the METRIC result of notebook 06. Canopy heights come from a crop table (illustrative values, as in notebook 04).

Running TSEB-PT

The ERA5 air temperature, vapour pressure and 10 m wind at the overpass drive the model; the sun zenith angle comes from the scene metadata. TSEB needs its forcing heights above the canopy, so over the 3.5 to 6 m orchards seb.tseb_pt treats the 2 m air temperature as the air 2 m above the tree tops (heights below hc + 2 m are raised to it), a common assumption with reanalysis forcing.

Over fallow land almost all net radiation reaches the soil and becomes soil heat and sensible heat. Transpiration dominates ET over dense canopies (T/ET of 0.86 for tomatoes at 91 % cover), and soil evaporation takes over as cover falls (0.44 for almonds, 0.31 for pistachios). Field studies with isotopes and sap flow in orchards and vineyards commonly report T/ET of 0.5 to 0.9 depending on cover and irrigation method (drip irrigation wets less soil than sprinklers or furrows). TSEB has no information on how the soil was irrigated: it infers soil evaporation only from the energy left after the soil’s sensible heat. The rangeland and developed land show the weakness: TSEB gives them about 165 W m-2 of soil evaporation, although their soil is dry in July. The soil resistance limits how much sensible heat the hot soil can lose, and the residual becomes evaporation. Soil evaporation is the least certain part of a TSEB result, and orchard T/ET here is likely too low.

Sensitivity to air temperature

ERA5 describes the air over a 25 km cell; the air over an irrigated orchard can be a few kelvin cooler than over the surrounding fallow land. Shifting the air temperature by up to 3 K in each direction shows how much that matters.

A 1 K warmer air temperature lowers Ts - Ta and Tc - Ta everywhere, which reduces sensible heat and raises LE across the scene: about 13 W m-2 per kelvin for the scene mean, roughly linearly. In relative terms fallow land is the most sensitive: its LE nearly triples from 45 to 123 W m-2 between -3 and +3 K. That is exactly why ALEXI and DisALEXI were developed: they replace the air temperature with quantities a satellite can measure, and why METRIC and SEBAL calibrate dT at anchors instead of using Ta at all.

Summary and exercises

  • TSEB splits radiometric temperature and net radiation between soil and canopy, starts the canopy at the Priestley-Taylor rate, and lowers it where the soil budget would need negative evaporation.
  • Soil and canopy temperatures differed by 9 to 18 K within the vegetated crop groups.
  • Transpiration dominates ET at full cover (T/ET 0.86 for tomatoes) and soil evaporation grows as cover falls; TSEB’s soil evaporation is the least certain part (it gave dry rangeland about 165 W m-2) because it knows nothing about how the soil was wetted.
  • TSEB agreed with METRIC on the pattern of latent heat (correlation 0.94) but changed the scene mean LE by about 13 W m-2 per kelvin of air temperature, the sensitivity that motivated ALEXI and DisALEXI.
  • Over tall canopies, reanalysis 2 m air temperature must be treated as air above the canopy, or the aerodynamic resistance is undefined.
