SEBAL: Solving for Sensible Heat with Hot and Cold Pixels

SEBAL, the Surface Energy Balance Algorithm for Land (Bastiaanssen et al. 1998), was the first energy balance model designed to run on a single satellite image with almost no ground data. Its central idea is to let the image calibrate itself. Somewhere in the scene there is a field so wet that all its available energy goes into evaporation, and somewhere a field so dry that none does. Between them, the near-surface temperature difference that drives sensible heat is assumed to change linearly with surface temperature. This notebook selects the two anchor pixels by hand and with the automated percentile method, runs the calibration and the stability iteration step by step, maps sensible heat, latent heat and the evaporative fraction, scales them to daily ET, and checks how much the result depends on the choice of anchors.

Overview

The SEBAL assumptions

  1. Linear dT. The temperature difference dT between 0.1 and 2 m above the surface is a linear function of LST: dT = a + b Ts. This replaces an unknown air temperature at every pixel with two numbers for the whole scene.
  2. Hot anchor. At a dry, bare pixel there is no evaporation: LE_hot = 0, so H_hot = Rn - G and dT_hot = H_hot rah_hot / (rho cp).
  3. Cold anchor. At a wet, well vegetated pixel all available energy evaporates: H_cold = 0, so dT_cold = 0.
  4. Constant evaporative fraction. EF = LE / (Rn - G) at the overpass holds for the whole day, so daily ET is EF Rn24 converted to mm, with Rn24 the 24 hour mean net radiation.

With the two anchors, b = (dT_hot - dT_cold) / (Ts_hot - Ts_cold) and a = dT_hot - b Ts_hot.

Why the iteration

rah depends on stability, stability on H, and H on dT, which depends on rah at the hot pixel. SEBAL starts neutral, computes a, b and H for every pixel, updates the Monin-Obukhov length and the corrections, recomputes rah and the anchor dT, and repeats until rah at the hot pixel stops changing (usually 5 to 10 iterations).

Why it works, and where it does not

The linear dT assumption is reasonable because both Ts and the near-surface air temperature are pulled up by the same thing, sensible heat. Because the calibration is internal, biases in LST (from atmospheric correction or emissivity) and in roughness are largely absorbed: what matters is the contrast between pixels, not their absolute temperature. The two anchor assumptions are the weak points:

  • In arid, advective regions such as the San Joaquin Valley in summer, well watered fields can evaporate more than Rn - G by drawing heat from the air (H < 0). Setting H_cold = 0 then underestimates ET of the wettest fields; METRIC (notebook 06) addresses this.
  • After rain a bare field still evaporates, so LE_hot = 0 overestimates H across the image.
  • The anchors must be representative: large, uniform fields, away from cloud edges, at similar elevation.

Automated anchor selection

Allen et al. (2013) automated anchor selection for METRIC with percentile rules, refined in eeMETRIC and geeSEBAL:

Anchor Pool Then
cold NDVI in the top 5 % of the image, albedo between 0.18 and 0.25 the coolest 20 % of that pool
hot NDVI in the bottom 10 %, between 0.10 and 0.28 (bare soil, not water) the hottest 20 % of that pool

From each pool GeoLattice keeps the candidates whose local LST standard deviation (3 x 3 pixels) is at or below the pool median, which removes field edges, and picks the pixel whose LST is closest to the pool mean. A land cover screen (for example, alfalfa for the cold pool and fallow for the hot pool) can narrow the pools further.

SEBAL with GeoLattice

Function Purpose

Learning objectives

  1. State the four SEBAL assumptions and derive a and b from the anchors.
  2. Select hot and cold anchors by hand and with the percentile method, and judge whether they are representative.
  3. Follow the stability iteration until the hot pixel’s rah converges.
  4. Map H, LE, the evaporative fraction and daily ET.
  5. Check the anchor constraints and find pixels where the assumptions break.
  6. Quantify how much daily ET depends on the choice of anchors.

Data

The 5 July 2024 Landsat 8 scene of the Westside San Joaquin Valley (sd.load_scene) with the net radiation, soil heat flux and roughness of notebooks 03 and 04 (sd.energy_inputs) and the ERA5 daily shortwave for Rn24.

Choosing the anchors

seb.select_anchors picks one pixel from each pool. For comparison, a manual pair is chosen the way an analyst would look for extremes: the coolest dense pixel (NDVI above 0.8) and the hottest fallow pixel, each inside a 7 x 7 pixel (210 m) window whose LST varies by less than 0.4 K, at least 300 m from the image edge.

Code 
auto = seb.select_anchors(p["ndvi"], p["lst"], albedo_=p["albedo"], candidates=cand)  # automated: closest to pool mean, homogeneous
ndvi_v, lst_v = p["ndvi"].values, p["lst"].values  # arrays
from scipy import ndimage  # local filters

f = np.nan_to_num(lst_v, nan=400.0)  # LST with gaps filled by an impossible value
sd7 = np.sqrt(np.maximum(ndimage.uniform_filter(f ** 2, 7) - ndimage.uniform_filter(f, 7) ** 2, 0))  # LST standard deviation in 7 x 7 windows

Both pairs sit in the expected corners of the scatter; the manual pair, chosen as extremes, lies just outside the main cloud of pixels (a tomato field at 33 C and a fallow field at 63 C). The automated cold anchor is not the very coolest pixel but a typical member of the coolest, densest pool, which is the intent: a single extreme pixel may be a wet spot, a canal or a cloud remnant. The automated run is used from here on; the last section measures how much this choice matters.

One calibration step by hand

The first SEBAL step assumes neutral air. With the hot and cold anchors:

Code 
hot, cold = auto["hot"], auto["cold"]  # the automated anchors
hy, hx, cy, cx = hot["iy"], hot["ix"], cold["iy"], cold["ix"]  # their rows and columns
u200, P = p["u200"], p["pressure"]  # wind at 200 m, pressure map
ustar = seb.friction_velocity(u200, 200.0, p["zom"])  # neutral u*
rah0 = seb.aerodynamic_resistance(ustar)  # neutral rah between 0.1 and 2 m
H_hot = float(p["rn"][hy, hx] - p["g"][hy, hx])  # LE = 0 at the hot pixel
rho_hot = float(seb.air_density(float(P[hy, hx]), hot["lst"]))  # a first guess of air density
dT_hot = H_hot * float(rah0[hy, hx]) / (rho_hot * seb.CP)  # K
a0, b0 = seb.calibrate_dt(hot["lst"], cold["lst"], dT_hot, 0.0)  # dT = a + b Ts with dT_cold = 0
print(f"hot pixel: Rn - G = {H_hot:.0f} W/m2, neutral rah = {float(rah0[hy, hx]):.1f} s/m, dT_hot = {dT_hot:.2f} K")  # hot anchor
print(f"dT = {a0:.2f} + {b0:.4f} Ts   (dT = 0 at Ts = {cold['lst'] - 273.15:.1f} C)")  # the line
hot pixel: Rn - G = 327 W/m2, neutral rah = 60.5 s/m, dT_hot = 18.94 K
dT = -235.50 + 0.7636 Ts   (dT = 0 at Ts = 35.2 C)

With neutral resistance the hot pixel needs a dT of about 19 K to carry all its available energy as sensible heat. That is too large: hot, dry surfaces are strongly unstable, so their real rah is much smaller and a smaller dT suffices. The iteration finds it.

The stability iteration

Summary and exercises

  • SEBAL calibrates dT = a + b Ts with a hot anchor (LE = 0) and a cold anchor (H = 0), and iterates rah and stability until the hot pixel converges.
  • The automated percentile pools found an orchard and a hay stubble field as anchors; a manual pair in large uniform fields gives similar values.
  • At the hot pixel, the stability iteration lowered rah from 60.5 s/m (neutral) to 17.2 s/m in five iterations, and dT_hot from about 19 K to 5.3 K.
  • SEBAL maps H, LE and EF well, but on this hot, advective day its daily ET for irrigated crops stays far below the alfalfa reference ET, because H_cold = 0 and the Rn24 scaling cannot represent energy drawn from the air.
  • Ten random anchor pairs from the pools moved the scene mean daily ET between 3.4 and 4.2 mm.
