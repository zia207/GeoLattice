Remote Sensing Fundamentals

Remote sensing is the measurement of an object’s properties without touching it, usually by recording electromagnetic (EM) energy reflected or emitted from the Earth’s surface.

Five notebooks in this part, then the chapters that follow, take that measurement from the sensor to a map, using geolattice.rsense.

What is remote sensing?

Remote sensing is the acquisition of information about an object or area from a distance, typically from aircraft, drones or satellites, by measuring electromagnetic radiation that the object reflects, emits or scatters back. A sensor never touches the surface: it records a number, and the science of remote sensing is turning that number into a physical quantity (reflectance, temperature, backscatter, height) and then into information (land cover, crop health, flood extent, ground motion).

The remote sensing process

Every remote sensing system, from a smartphone camera to a radar satellite, follows the same chain (Lillesand, Kiefer & Chipman, 2015; Jensen, 2007):

Step What happens Example
A. Energy source The Sun (passive) or the sensor itself (active) illuminates the target Sunlight; a radar pulse
B. Path to the target Radiation passes through the atmosphere and is partly scattered and absorbed Blue light scattered by air molecules
C. Interaction with the target The surface reflects, absorbs, transmits and emits energy depending on its properties Leaves reflect near-infrared strongly
D. Path to the sensor The returning signal crosses the atmosphere again Haze adds “path radiance”
E. Recording The sensor converts radiance into digital numbers 12-bit Landsat OLI counts
F. Transmission and processing Data are downlinked, calibrated, geolocated and corrected Level-1 → Level-2 products
G. Interpretation and analysis Physical values become information NDVI, land-cover maps, change maps
H. Application Information supports a decision Irrigation scheduling, disaster response

A short history

Year Milestone
1858 Gaspard-Félix Tournachon (“Nadar”) takes the first aerial photograph, from a balloon over Paris
1909 to 1918 Aerial photography from aircraft; systematic military reconnaissance in World War I
1940s Radar and colour-infrared film developed during World War II
1960 TIROS-1, the first weather satellite; the term “remote sensing” is coined (Evelyn Pruitt, U.S. Office of Naval Research)
1972 Landsat 1 (ERTS-1) begins the longest continuous record of Earth’s land surface
1978 Seasat, the first civilian spaceborne synthetic aperture radar (SAR)
1999 Terra/MODIS, daily global multispectral observation; IKONOS, first commercial sub-metre satellite
2014 to 2017 Copernicus Sentinel-1 (SAR) and Sentinel-2 (multispectral) with free and open data
2018 to 2019 GEDI and ICESat-2 bring spaceborne lidar for forest structure and elevation
2020s Small-satellite constellations, hyperspectral missions (EnMAP, EMIT, PRISMA) and L-band SAR (NISAR)

The open-data policies of Landsat (2008) and Copernicus (2014) turned remote sensing into a data-science discipline: petabytes of analysis-ready imagery are now searched through STAC catalogues and processed in the cloud, which is the workflow geolattice.rsense is built for.

Physical principles

The electromagnetic spectrum

EM energy travels as waves; the wavelength λ and frequency ν are linked by the speed of light, \(c = \lambda \nu\), and each photon carries energy \(E = h\nu = hc/\lambda\). Shorter wavelengths therefore mean more energetic photons.

Remote sensing uses several broad regions of the spectrum:

Region Wavelength What sensors measure Examples
Visible (VIS) 0.4 to 0.7 µm Reflected sunlight Landsat B2 to B4, Sentinel-2 B2 to B4
Near infrared (NIR) 0.7 to 1.3 µm Reflected sunlight; very bright for leaves Landsat B5, Sentinel-2 B8
Shortwave infrared (SWIR) 1.3 to 3 µm Reflected sunlight; sensitive to water content and minerals Landsat B6 to B7, Sentinel-2 B11 to B12
Mid-wave infrared (MWIR) 3 to 8 µm Mixed reflected and emitted energy; very hot targets MODIS fire bands (3.9 µm)
Thermal infrared (TIR) 8 to 14 µm Energy emitted by the surface (temperature) Landsat B10 to B11, MODIS LST
Microwave 1 mm-1 m Active radar echoes or passive emission Sentinel-1 (C-band, 5.6 cm), NISAR (L-band, 24 cm)

A blue photon carries about 100,000 times more energy than a C-band radar photon. That is why optical detectors count photons with semiconductors while radar receivers measure the amplitude and phase of a wave, and why microwave radiation passes through clouds that block visible light.

Blackbody radiation: why the Sun and the Earth shine in different bands

Every object above absolute zero emits radiation. For an ideal emitter (a blackbody) the spectral radiance at temperature \(T\) follows Planck’s law:

\[B_\lambda(T) = \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5} \, \frac{1}{e^{hc/(\lambda k_B T)} - 1}\]

Two consequences follow:

  • Wien’s displacement law: the wavelength of peak emission is \(\lambda_{max} = 2898\,\mu m\,K / T\).
  • Stefan-Boltzmann law: the total emitted power grows as \(M = \varepsilon\sigma T^4\), where \(\sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}\,W\,m^{-2}K^{-4}\) and \(\varepsilon \le 1\) is the emissivity. Real surfaces are grey bodies with \(\varepsilon\) between about 0.9 (dry soil) and 0.99 (water), which is why thermal remote sensing must correct for emissivity.

Comparing the Sun (≈5,778 K) with the Earth’s surface (≈300 K) explains the split between reflective and emissive remote sensing.

# This code compares the blackbody radiation curves (spectral radiance) of the Sun and the Earth,
# and illustrates why they emit at very different wavelengths.

# Boltzmann constant in J/K
kB = 1.381e-23

# Define a range of wavelengths in meters: from 0.1 µm to 100 µm (for plotting)
wl = np.logspace(-7, -4, 2000)  # 1e-7 m = 0.1 µm, 1e-4 m = 100 µm

# Planck's law: returns spectral radiance B_lambda for wavelength(s) wl [m] and temperature T [K]
def planck(wl, T):
    # The formula returns radiance in W m⁻² sr⁻¹ m⁻¹
    return 2 * h * c**2 / wl**5 / (np.exp(h * c / (wl * kB * T)) - 1)

# Get the radiance curves for the Sun (5778 K) and the Earth (300 K)
# The Sun's radiance is scaled to represent the apparent radiance at Earth's distance (by solid angle factor)
sun = planck(wl, 5778) * (6.96e8 / 1.496e11) ** 2     # (Sun radius / Earth-Sun distance)^2
earth = planck(wl, 300)  # Planck curve of the Earth's surface at 300 K

# Plot both curves on a log-log plot (wavelength in microns)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 4.5))
# sunlight on log-log axes (wavelength in µm)
ax.loglog(wl * 1e6, sun, label="Sunlight at the top of the atmosphere (5,778 K)")
ax.loglog(wl * 1e6, earth, label="Earth's surface (300 K)")  # Earth emission

The atmosphere: scattering, absorption and windows

Radiation crosses the atmosphere twice in passive optical remote sensing (Sun → surface → sensor). Two processes change it on the way.

Scattering redirects radiation. Its strength depends on the size of the particle relative to the wavelength:

Type Particles Wavelength dependence Visible effect
Rayleigh Gas molecules (≪ λ) \(\propto \lambda^{-4}\) Blue sky; haze strongest in blue bands
Mie Aerosols, smoke, dust (≈ λ) roughly \(\lambda^{-1}\) to \(\lambda^{0}\) Hazy, washed-out images
Non-selective Cloud droplets, ice (≫ λ) none White clouds

Scattered light that reaches the sensor without touching the surface is path radiance. It brightens dark targets, especially in the blue, and lowers image contrast. Atmospheric correction (the Processing levels: from DN to physical units section) removes it.

Absorption removes energy at specific wavelengths: ozone (O₃) in the ultraviolet, water vapour (H₂O) near 0.94, 1.14, 1.38, 1.87 and 2.7 µm and above 5 µm, carbon dioxide (CO₂) near 2.0, 4.3 and 15 µm, and oxygen (O₂) at 0.76 µm. The spectral regions where the atmosphere is mostly transparent are the atmospheric windows, and sensor bands are placed inside them:

Window Used by
0.3 to 1.3 µm (VIS-NIR) All multispectral sensors
1.5 to 1.8 µm and 2.0 to 2.4 µm (SWIR) Landsat B6/B7, Sentinel-2 B11/B12
3.5 to 4.2 µm (MWIR) Fire detection (MODIS, VIIRS)
8 to 14 µm (TIR), split by ozone at 9.6 µm Landsat B10/B11, ECOSTRESS
> 1 cm (microwave) Radar; almost unaffected by clouds

The opposite trick is also useful: bands placed inside an absorption feature measure the absorber itself. Landsat 8 band 9 (1.37 µm) sits in a strong water-vapour band, so the ground is invisible and only high cirrus clouds appear bright.

Energy-matter interactions at the surface

Radiation reaching the surface is split three ways. By conservation of energy, at each wavelength

\[E_I(\lambda) = E_R(\lambda) + E_A(\lambda) + E_T(\lambda),\]

the incident energy equals the reflected, absorbed and transmitted parts. Dividing by \(E_I\) gives the reflectance, absorptance and transmittance, which sum to one. Remote sensing of reflected energy measures the spectral reflectance

\[\rho(\lambda) = \frac{E_R(\lambda)}{E_I(\lambda)},\]

and the curve of \(\rho\) against wavelength is the material’s spectral signature (the Spectral signatures from real images section). Kirchhoff’s law links absorption and emission: a surface in thermal equilibrium emits as well as it absorbs at each wavelength (\(\varepsilon_\lambda = \alpha_\lambda\)), so for an opaque surface \(\varepsilon_\lambda = 1 - \rho_\lambda\). That is why water, a poor reflector in the thermal infrared, is an almost perfect emitter.

The geometry of reflection matters too:

  • A specular surface (calm water, smooth roofs) reflects like a mirror. It looks either very dark or, at the mirror angle, dazzlingly bright (sun glint).
  • A Lambertian (perfectly diffuse) surface reflects equally in all directions, so its radiance does not depend on viewing angle. Most land surfaces are treated as approximately Lambertian.
  • Real surfaces lie in between. Their directional behaviour is described by the bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BRDF): forests look brighter with the Sun behind the observer (the “hot spot”) because shadows are hidden.

For microwaves the controlling properties are different: surface roughness relative to the wavelength, dielectric constant (mainly moisture) and geometry (corner reflectors in cities, volume scattering in canopies). The radar notebooks (07_03) develop these for SAR.

Radiometric quantities and the radiative transfer equation

Quantity Symbol Unit Meaning
Radiant flux Φ W Energy per unit time
Irradiance E W m⁻² Flux arriving on a surface
Exitance M W m⁻² Flux leaving a surface
Radiance L W m⁻² sr⁻¹ µm⁻¹ Flux per area, per solid angle, per wavelength: what a sensor measures
Reflectance ρ , Ratio of reflected to incident flux (0 to 1)

For a Lambertian surface the radiance reaching a sensor at the top of the atmosphere is

\[L_{sensor}(\lambda) = \frac{\rho(\lambda)\,E_{sun}(\lambda)\cos\theta_s\,T_{\downarrow}(\lambda)\,T_{\uparrow}(\lambda)}{\pi\,d^2} + L_{path}(\lambda),\]

with exo-atmospheric solar irradiance \(E_{sun}\), solar zenith angle \(\theta_s\), Earth-Sun distance \(d\) (astronomical units), downward and upward atmospheric transmittances \(T\), and path radiance \(L_{path}\). Setting \(T = 1\) and \(L_{path} = 0\) and solving for \(\rho\) gives the top-of-atmosphere (TOA) reflectance

\[\rho_{TOA} = \frac{\pi\,L_{sensor}\,d^2}{E_{sun}\cos\theta_s}.\]

Estimating \(T\) and \(L_{path}\) (from radiative transfer models, or from dark objects in the image) gives surface reflectance, the starting point of most analysis. The Physics in the data: the Sun’s spectrum and atmospheric scattering section uses this equation to recover the solar spectrum from a real Landsat metadata file.

Spectral signatures from real images

A spectral signature is how reflectance changes with wavelength for a material:

  • Water reflects a little blue and green light and absorbs almost all NIR and SWIR, so it is dark beyond the visible.
  • Healthy vegetation absorbs blue and red light for photosynthesis, reflects some green, and reflects NIR very strongly because of the internal structure of leaves. The sharp rise between red and NIR is the red edge. Leaf water absorbs in the SWIR.
  • Built surfaces and bare soil have flatter spectra that rise gradually with wavelength.

Physics in the data: the Sun’s spectrum and atmospheric scattering

Two theoretical results from the Physical principles section can be checked against the Landsat scene.

The solar spectrum hidden in the metadata

The MTL file gives two linear conversions for each band: DN to radiance (\(L = M_L\,DN + A_L\)) and DN to reflectance without the sun-angle term (\(\rho' = M_\rho\,DN + A_\rho\)). Both describe the same pixel, so the radiative transfer equation of the Radiometric quantities and the radiative transfer equation section, \(\rho' = \pi L d^2 / E_{sun}\), lets us solve for the exo-atmospheric solar irradiance each band sees:

\[E_{sun} = \frac{\pi\,L\,d^2}{\rho'}.\]

Planck’s law for a 5,778 K Sun predicts the same quantity: \(E_\lambda = \pi B_\lambda(T)\,(R_{sun}/d_{AU})^2\).

Code 
# --- Explanation of the code below ---
# The following code section computes and compares the exo-atmospheric solar irradiance values used in a Landsat 8 scene's metadata (the MTL file)
# to theoretical values computed from Planck's law for a blackbody at the Sun's temperature (~5,778 K).
# This checks how closely the calibration metadata matches the physical solar spectrum.

# 1. Define the center wavelengths (in microns) for each OLI/Landsat 8 band.
OLI_CENTRE = {
    "coastal": 0.443,
    "blue": 0.482,
    "green": 0.561,
    "red": 0.655,
    "nir": 0.865,
    "swir16": 1.609,
    "swir22": 2.201,
}

# 2. Get the Earth-Sun distance for the day of imaging, from the MTL metadata.
d = mtl["EARTH_SUN_DISTANCE"]

# 3. Recover E_sun (exo-atmospheric solar irradiance) for each band using metadata calibration coefficients.
esun = {}
for b, name in REFLECTIVE.items():  # every reflective band
    if name not in OLI_CENTRE:  # skip bands without a centre wavelength
        continue
    # Choose any digital number (DN) value (here 20,000) since the ratio is linear; it cancels out.
    dn_ref = 20000.0
    # Compute radiance at the top of atmosphere using radiometric rescaling.
    L = mtl[f"RADIANCE_MULT_BAND_{b}"] * dn_ref + mtl[f"RADIANCE_ADD_BAND_{b}"]
    # Compute reflectance without correcting for sun angle.
    rho = mtl[f"REFLECTANCE_MULT_BAND_{b}"] * dn_ref + mtl[f"REFLECTANCE_ADD_BAND_{b}"]
    # Rearranged radiative transfer equation gives E_sun.
    esun[name] = np.pi * L * d**2 / rho
# Store these values in a pandas Series.
esun = pd.Series(esun, name="E_sun from MTL (W m⁻² µm⁻¹)")

# 4. Compute the theoretical solar irradiance at each band's wavelength using Planck's law (blackbody at 5,778 K).
#    - Converts wavelengths to meters, applies πB_λ(T), multiplies by the (Sun radius/distance)^2 solid angle, and scales units.

)

Rayleigh scattering in the darkest pixels

The darkest pixels of a scene reflect almost nothing at the surface, so most of their TOA signal is atmospheric path radiance. If molecular (Rayleigh) scattering dominates, the dark-object reflectance should fall steeply with wavelength. The dark object subtraction method (DOS1, Chavez 1996; rs.optical.dos1) uses exactly this signal for a first-order atmospheric correction.

The method needs genuinely dark targets in every band it corrects: deep clear water or deep shadow. This farmland subset has no lake, and in the visible bands the darkest pixels are dense crops and woodland, which absorb blue and red light for photosynthesis. In the NIR and SWIR the same vegetation is bright, so the darkest pixels there are not dark at all. We therefore test the scattering law on the visible bands only.

Code

The four resolutions

Every sensor is a compromise between four kinds of resolution:

Resolution Question it answers Landsat 8/9 Sentinel-2 MODIS
Spatial How small an object can be seen? 30 m (15 m pan, 100 m thermal) 10, 20, 60 m 250, 500, 1000 m
Spectral How many bands, and how narrow? 11 bands 13 bands 36 bands
Temporal How often is a place revisited? 16 days (8 with both) 5 days (two satellites) 1 to 2 days
Radiometric How finely is brightness quantized? 12-bit (Landsat 9: 14-bit), stored 16-bit 12-bit 12-bit

Improving one usually costs another: MODIS sees the whole planet daily because its pixels are large, while Sentinel-2’s 10 m pixels need five days to cover the globe.

Temporal resolution

Temporal resolution cannot be seen in a single scene, but it decides what can be monitored. Crop growth needs observations every few days to weeks, floods need hours to days, and cloud cover reduces the usable revisit: the full Landsat scene used here was 18.8% cloudy (this subset happens to be clear), and in humid regions a 16-day revisit often yields only one or two clear views a month. Combining sensors (Landsat + Sentinel-2 in the Harmonized Landsat Sentinel-2 product, rs.optical.load_hls), adding SAR, or fusing coarse daily data with fine sparse data (rs.fusion.starfm) are the usual remedies. The time-series notebooks (07_06) cover time-series analysis.

Passive and active sensors

Passive sensors record natural energy: reflected sunlight (optical) or emitted heat (thermal). Everything in the sections from Spectral signatures from real images to The four resolutions is passive.

Active sensors send their own pulse and time the echo:

  • Radar (SAR) transmits microwaves that pass through clouds and work at night, which is why Sentinel-1 is the workhorse for flood mapping in the monsoon. It measures backscatter, usually expressed in decibels (dB).
  • Lidar transmits laser pulses and records return times, giving precise 3-D point clouds of terrain and vegetation structure.

Radar backscatter spans several orders of magnitude, so it is usually shown in decibels, \(\sigma^0_{dB} = 10 \log_{10}(\sigma^0)\):

Processing levels: from DN to physical units

Raw sensor counts pass through a chain of corrections before analysis:

Level Content Example
L0 Raw instrument data Downlinked telemetry
L1 Calibrated, geolocated radiance or TOA reflectance (as DN with scale factors) Landsat Collection 1/2 Level-1, Sentinel-2 L1C
L2 Geophysical variables: surface reflectance, surface temperature, backscatter Landsat C2 Level-2, Sentinel-2 L2A, MOD09
L3 Gridded, composited or aggregated products MOD13 16-day NDVI
L4 Model outputs that combine data GPP, soil moisture analyses

For Landsat Level-1 the conversions use coefficients from the scene’s metadata (*_MTL.txt):

  • Radiance: \(L_\lambda = M_L \cdot DN + A_L\) (rs.optical.radiance)
  • TOA reflectance: \(\rho = (M_\rho \cdot DN + A_\rho) / \sin(\theta_{SE})\), with sun elevation \(\theta_{SE}\) (rs.optical.toa_reflectance)
  • Brightness temperature (thermal bands): \(BT = K_2 / \ln(K_1/L_\lambda + 1)\) (rs.optical.brightness_temperature), the inverse of Planck’s law for the band.

Thermal band: brightness temperature

Band 10 (10.9 µm) records emitted radiance. Inverting Planck’s law with the band constants \(K_1\) and \(K_2\) from the MTL file gives the brightness temperature: the temperature a blackbody would need to produce the measured radiance. Correcting for emissivity (estimated from NDVI, rs.optical.emissivity_ndvi) turns it into land surface temperature (rs.optical.landsat_lst, optical notebooks 07_02).

Summary and exercises

Key ideas

  • Remote sensing turns measured radiance into physical quantities and then into information, through a chain of source, atmosphere, target, sensor and processing.
  • Optical sensors (0.4 to 3 µm) measure reflected sunlight; thermal sensors (8 to 14 µm) measure emitted energy; radar and lidar supply their own illumination.
  • The atmosphere scatters (Rayleigh ∝ λ⁻⁴) and absorbs; sensors look through its windows, and atmospheric correction removes path radiance.
  • Spectral signatures (water, vegetation, built surfaces) are the basis of every spectral index and classifier.
  • Spatial, spectral, temporal and radiometric resolution trade off against each other.
  • Analysis-ready data are Level-2 surface reflectance, surface temperature or calibrated backscatter in physical units.
  • geolattice.rsense covers the whole workflow, from data access to deep learning, on CPU or GPU.

What the data showed

  • Sentinel-2 ground truth reproduced the textbook signatures, including the red edge (grass: 0.105 → 0.275 → 0.383 from 705 to 842 nm).
  • The Landsat 8 metadata alone recovered the solar spectrum, within 2 to 18% of a 5,778 K blackbody.
  • Path radiance in the visible bands fell as λ^−2.75, between Rayleigh and aerosol scattering. DOS1 removed most of it, but should not be trusted in bands without dark targets.
  • Bare fields were 2.7 °C warmer than transpiring vegetation in brightness temperature.
  • Red-edge and SWIR bands separated trees from grass four to six times better than the visible bands.
