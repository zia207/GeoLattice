Texture Analysis of Landsat Imagery: Transform-Based (Spectral) Methods

A texture is a pattern that repeats at certain spatial frequencies and in certain directions.

Overview

Transform- based methods make frequency, orientation and scale explicit by moving the image into another domain: the Fourier domain, a bank of Gabor filters, or a wavelet decomposition. This notebook explains the theory of the three transforms, computes them on the NIR band of a Landsat 9 scene over Las Vegas with geolattice.rsense.texture, maps the dominant orientation of the street grid and the mountain ridges, and tests what transform-based texture adds to spectral information.

Image texture in brief

Spectral values tell us what a pixel is made of; texture tells us how pixel values are arranged around it. Texture is the spatial variation of gray levels inside a neighbourhood and describes properties such as smoothness, coarseness, regularity, directionality and contrast. Haralick, Shanmugam and Dinstein (1973) noted that tone and texture are always present together in an image, but one may dominate the other depending on the scene.

At Landsat’s 30 m resolution many land-cover classes overlap spectrally: urban fabric, bare soil and rock are all bright and dry in an arid scene; crop types or forest stands can share the same NDVI; rough terrain mixes lit slopes and shadow within a few pixels. Texture separates such classes because it responds to pattern, not only to brightness, and texture layers are routinely stacked with bands and indices as predictors in land-cover classification, urban mapping, forest-structure modelling and soil mapping.

Methods are usually grouped into six families. This series covers them in five notebooks:

All methods come from geolattice.rsense.texture. Every function accepts a 2-D xr.DataArray (or NumPy array) and returns a georeferenced result with the same shape as the input; inputs with leading dimensions (band, time, polarization) are processed slice by slice, and multi-feature methods return an xr.Dataset with one variable per feature.

Theory: texture as frequency, orientation and scale

1. Fourier power spectrum. The 2-D discrete Fourier transform writes an \(M \times M\) tile \(g(x, y)\) as a sum of sinusoids:

\[F(u, v) = \sum_{x=0}^{M-1}\sum_{y=0}^{M-1} g(x, y)\, e^{-2\pi i (ux + vy)/M}.\]

The power spectrum \(|F(u,v)|^2\) tells how much variance the tile has at each spatial frequency \((u, v)\). The distance from the centre of the (centred) spectrum, \(\rho = \sqrt{u^2 + v^2}\), is the spatial frequency (fine patterns far from the centre, coarse patterns near it), and the angle \(\phi = \arctan(v/u)\) is the orientation of the pattern (perpendicular to the stripes). Summing the power in concentric rings gives a rotation-invariant description of coarseness (Rosenfeld and Troy 1970; Weszka, Dyer and Rosenfeld 1976):

\[E_k = \frac{\sum_{\rho \in \text{ring } k} |F|^2}{\sum |F|^2}.\]

Each tile is multiplied by a 2-D Hann window before the transform, to suppress the artificial edges created by cutting the tile out of the image (spectral leakage).

2. Gabor filters. A Gabor filter is a sinusoid of frequency \(f\) and orientation \(\theta\) under a Gaussian envelope (Gabor 1946; Daugman 1985):

\[h(x, y) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{x'^2 + y'^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\exp\!\left(2\pi i f x'\right),\qquad x' = x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta,\; y' = -x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta.\]

Convolving the image with \(h\) gives a complex response whose energy \(|g * h|^2\) is high where the image contains a pattern of that frequency and orientation. A bank of filters (several \(f\) and \(\theta\)) is a localized Fourier analysis: unlike the global spectrum of a tile, every pixel gets its own frequency-orientation description. Gabor filters resemble the receptive fields of simple cells in the mammalian visual cortex, which is one reason they work well for texture segmentation (Jain and Farrokhnia 1991).

3. Wavelets. The discrete wavelet transform (Mallat 1989) splits an image into an approximation and detail at successive scales. One level of the 2-D Haar transform takes each 2 × 2 block \(\{a, b; c, d\}\) and returns

\[LL = \tfrac{a+b+c+d}{2},\quad LH = \tfrac{a-b+c-d}{2},\quad HL = \tfrac{a+b-c-d}{2},\quad HH = \tfrac{a-b-c+d}{2},\]

each at half the resolution. LL is a smoothed image that feeds the next level; LH and HL hold detail along the two image axes and HH diagonal detail. Level 1 describes 30 m detail, level 2 60 m, level 3 120 m, and so on. The local energy of each subband is a scale- and orientation-specific texture feature.

Transform Localization Frequency resolution Orientation Cost
Fourier rings One tile at a time Fine (many rings) Averaged out (rings) Low (FFT)
Gabor bank Every pixel Few chosen frequencies Explicit, chosen angles Medium (one convolution per filter)
Haar wavelet Every pixel Octaves (×2 per level) 2 axes + diagonal Very low

Loading the Landsat bands and the study window

The files hold raw Collection 2 Level-2 digital numbers (DN). We read them with rs.read, put them in one xr.Dataset, and convert them to surface reflectance with rs.optical.scale, which applies reflectance = DN × 0.0000275 − 0.2 and turns the fill value 0 into NaN.

Window-based texture is easier to see, and much faster to compute, on a smaller area. We work on a 512 × 512 pixel window (15.4 × 15.4 km) on the eastern edge of Las Vegas that contains four very different surfaces:

  • the regular street grid of the city (west),
  • the smooth, bare alluvial fan below Frenchman Mountain (centre),
  • the folded sedimentary ridges of Frenchman Mountain (east),
  • the vegetated channel of the Las Vegas Wash (south), red in colour infrared.
Code

Fourier power spectrum and ring energy

Reading a power spectrum: synthetic stripes

Three synthetic 64 × 64 images with stripes of period 16, 8 and 4 pixels, and one with random noise. The spectrum of a stripe pattern is a pair of bright points; their distance from the centre grows as the period shrinks, and they lie on a line perpendicular to the stripes.

Code 
# Synthetic textures and their Fourier power spectra.
yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:64, 0:64]  # row and column index of a 64 x 64 grid
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)  # random generator with a fixed seed
synth = {  # four test images
    "period 16, vertical stripes": np.sin(2 * np.pi * xx / 16),  # brightness varies along x only
    "period 8, diagonal stripes": np.sin(2 * np.pi * (xx + yy) / (8 * np.sqrt(2))),  # varies along x + y
    "period 4, horizontal stripes": np.sin(2 * np.pi * yy / 4),  # varies along y only, at a high frequency
    "random noise": rng.normal(size=(64, 64)),  # no structure at all
}
han = np.outer(np.hanning(64), np.hanning(64))  # 2-D Hann window: tapers the edges to avoid spectral leakage
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 4, figsize=(16, 8))  # images (top) and spectra (bottom)
for k, (name, img) in enumerate(synth.items()):  # one column per image
    # power = |FFT|² of the tapered, mean-free image; fftshift puts frequency 0 in the centre
    spec = np.abs(np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fft2((img - img.mean()) * han)))**2
    axes[0, k].imshow(img, cmap="gray"); axes[0, k].set_title(name, fontsize=10)  # the image
    # the spectrum on a log scale
    axes[1, k].imshow(np.log10(spec + 1e-6), cmap="magma"); axes[1, k].set_title("log10 power spectrum", fontsize=10)
for a in axes.ravel():  # no ticks
    a.set_xticks([]); a.set_yticks([])
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()  # arrange and draw

Each stripe pattern gives a pair of bright points in the spectrum, on a line perpendicular to the stripes. The period-16 points sit close to the centre; the period-4 points sit far out. Noise spreads its power over the whole spectrum.

Code 
# Ring energy of the same images, computed with tx.fourier_texture (32 x 32 tiles, 6 rings).
# mean share of energy in each of 6 frequency rings, per image
ring_tab = pd.DataFrame({name: {k: float(np.nanmean(v)) for k, v in tx.fourier_texture(img, size=32, step=8, n_rings=6).items()}
                         for name, img in synth.items()})  # one column per synthetic image
ax = ring_tab.plot(kind="bar", figsize=(10, 4), rot=0)  # grouped bars: rings on x, one colour per image
ax.set(ylabel="Share of spectral energy", xlabel="Ring (0 = coarsest, 5 = finest)",  # axis labels
       title="Fourier ring energy of synthetic textures")  # and title
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()  # arrange and draw
# show the table
ring_tab.round(3)

ring0 1.0 0.415 0.000 0.046
ring1 0.0 0.585 0.177 0.122
ring2 0.0 0.000 0.823 0.217
ring3 0.0 0.000 0.000 0.317
ring4 0.0 0.000 0.000 0.234
ring5 0.0 0.000 0.000 0.064

Period 16 (2 cycles per 32-pixel tile) falls entirely in ring0, period 8 (4 cycles) mostly in ring1 (the diagonal pattern leaks into ring0), and period 4 (8 cycles) in ring2. Noise spreads across all rings. The rings extend to the corners of the spectrum, beyond the highest frequency along the axes, so ring5 collects only the finest diagonal frequencies and always holds a small share.

Note that the energy fractions include the tile mean (the zero frequency), which always falls in ring0. On real imagery, where most variance is at low frequencies, ring0 therefore holds most of the energy, and the information is in how much energy spills into the outer rings.

Gabor filter bank

The filters

Below are the real parts of the 12 kernels used in this section: 3 frequencies × 4 orientations. Frequency is in cycles per pixel, so f = 0.1 is a 10-pixel (300 m) period, f = 0.25 a 4-pixel (120 m) period and f = 0.4 a 2.5-pixel (75 m) period. In scikit-image, the wave of a filter with θ = 0 runs along the x-axis, so it responds to vertical stripes (north-south streets); θ = 90 responds to horizontal stripes.

Application: orientation mapping

The four orientations of the Gabor bank can be combined into two maps for one frequency:

  • Dominant orientation: the θ with the highest energy.
  • Anisotropy: (max − min) / (max + min) of the energies across θ, from 0 (isotropic: equal energy in all directions) to 1 (all energy in one direction).

Orientation is meaningful only where texture is both strong and anisotropic, so the map is masked where energy or anisotropy is low.

Code 
f = "0p25"  # use the f = 0.25 filters

emax, emin = E.max("theta"), E.min("theta")  # strongest and weakest orientation response
aniso = (emax - emin) / (emax + emin + 1e-12)  # 0 = same in every direction, 1 = one direction only

def box_means(ds):  # average feature values per sample box
    """Mean of every variable of `ds` inside each sample box."""
    # rows = features, columns = boxes
    return pd.DataFrame({name: {v: float(ds[v].isel(y=ys, x=xs).mean()) for v in ds.data_vars}
                         for name, (ys, xs) in BOXES.items()})

def plot_signatures(sig, title):  # compare the boxes feature by feature
    """Bar chart of box means, min-max scaled per feature across the three boxes."""
    # scale each feature to 0-1 across the three boxes
    norm = sig.sub(sig.min(axis=1), axis=0).div(sig.max(axis=1) - sig.min(axis=1), axis=0)
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(max(7, 0.75 * len(norm)), 4.5))  # width grows with the number of features
    x = np.arange(len(norm))  # one group of bars per feature
    for k, name in enumerate(norm.columns):  # one bar per box, shifted sideways
        ax.bar(x + (k - 1) * 0.27, norm[name], width=0.27, color=COLORS[name], label=name)  # draw the bars
    ax.set_xticks(x, norm.index, rotation=45, ha="right")  # feature names, rotated
    ax.set(ylabel="Relative value (0 = lowest box, 1 = highest)", title=title)  # labels
    ax.legend(); plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()  # legend and draw

def kmeans_map(ds, k=5, n_fit=40000, seed=0):  # unsupervised map from a set of layers
    """Standardize the layers, fit k-means on a random pixel sample and label every pixel."""
    df = ds.to_dataframe(dim_order=["y", "x"])[list(ds.data_vars)]  # pixels as rows, layers as columns
    ok = df.notna().all(axis=1).values  # pixels without missing values
    X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(df[ok].values)  # standardise so every layer weighs the same
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)  # random sample generator
    # fit on at most 40,000 random pixels (faster)
    km = KMeans(k, n_init=10, random_state=seed).fit(X[rng.choice(len(X), min(n_fit, len(X)), replace=False)])
    lab = np.full(ok.size, np.nan); lab[ok] = km.predict(X)  # label all valid pixels; others stay NaN
    # labels back on the image grid
    return xr.DataArray(lab.reshape(ds.sizes["y"], ds.sizes["x"]), coords={"y": ds.y, "x": ds.x}, dims=("y", "x"))

def speckle(lab):  # how noisy a cluster map is
    """Share of pixels whose label differs from the majority label of their 3 x 3 neighbourhood."""
    a = np.nan_to_num(lab.values, nan=-1).astype(int)  # NaN -> -1 so the labels are integers
    # majority label in each 3 x 3 window
    mode = generic_filter(a, lambda v: np.bincount(v.astype(int) + 1).argmax() - 1, size=3)
    return float((a != mode).mean())  # share of pixels that differ from their neighbourhood majority

def box_purity(lab):  # how uniform each box is in a cluster map
    """Share of each box's pixels that fall in the box's most common cluster."""
    out = {}  # one value per box
    for name, (ys, xs) in BOXES.items():  # loop over the boxes
        v = lab.isel(y=ys, x=xs).values.ravel()  # labels inside the box
        v = v[np.isfinite(v)].astype(int)  # drop missing labels
        out[name] = np.bincount(v).max() / v.size  # share of the most common label
    return out  # dictionary box -> purity

Texture is useful only if it carries information that the spectral bands do not. We run k-means with 5 clusters on (a) the four bands and NDVI only and (b) the same spectral layers plus this notebook’s texture features, all standardized. Two numbers summarize the maps:

Summary and exercises

Key points

  • Transform-based methods describe texture by frequency (how fine), orientation (which way) and scale (at which resolution).
  • The Fourier spectrum of a tile shows the period and direction of repeating patterns; ring energy is a rotation-invariant summary of coarseness. Tile size trades frequency resolution against spatial detail.
  • A Gabor bank gives every pixel its own frequency-orientation description and can map the dominant direction of streets, ridges, crop rows or dunes.
  • Haar wavelets split detail into octaves of scale and three orientations at almost no computational cost; level 1 captures 30 m detail, level 2 60 m and level 3 120 m.
  • Most transform energies are strongly skewed; use their logarithms in statistics and models.

Exercises

  1. Add θ = 22.5°, 67.5°, 112.5° and 157.5° to the Gabor bank. Does the orientation map of the ridges become more precise?
  2. Use tx.fourier_texture with 12 rings on 64 × 64 tiles and plot the mean ring spectrum of each sample box. At what period does the city peak?
  3. Compute wavelet texture to level 4 (240 m). At which level do the city and the ridges differ most?
  4. Repeat the orientation mapping on the red band. Are the dominant orientations the same?
  5. Apply tx.gabor_texture to an agricultural Landsat scene with centre-pivot or row crops, and map the row direction of each field.
