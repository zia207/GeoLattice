RGB + NIR Multispectral Analysis

Adding a near-infrared camera to an RGB drone unlocks NDVI and its relatives: healthy leaves reflect NIR strongly and absorb red, a contrast no visible-band index can match.

Overview

A two-camera rig brings two problems a single multispectral sensor hides. The cameras see slightly different footprints, so the bands must be co-registered, and they record raw digital numbers (DN), not reflectance, so indices are relative unless the images are calibrated against reference panels. This notebook works through both on the paired Canola, Drybean, Wheat and Lentil-Wheat crop sets, then follows NDVI through the season for each crop.

Core concepts

Learning objectives

  1. Measure and remove the misalignment between NIR and RGB frames with feature matching (ORB/SIFT + RANSAC homography) and ECC refinement (rs.uav.coregister).
  2. Compute NDVI, GNDVI and SAVI from a registered pair and compare NDVI with ExG.
  3. Explain relative vs calibrated reflectance and apply panel calibration, the empirical line method and irradiance normalisation (rs.uav.panel_calibration, empirical_line, sunshine_normalize).
  4. Build NDVI trajectories across flight dates for four crops.

Data

Data Type
Paired RGB/NIR PNG crops: Canola (4 dates), Drybean (6), Wheat (3), Lentil-Wheat (2) Real, relative DN (no panels, GSD or licence on file)
Calibration-panel example in Section 5 Simulated

Co-registration

rs.uav.coregister(moving, reference) fits the homography (ORB or SIFT features, Lowe’s ratio test, RANSAC), optionally refines it with the Enhanced Correlation Coefficient (ECC) method (kept only if it improves the match), and warps the moving band. load_pair(..., register=True) does the same inside the loader.

Pixel correlation between NIR and green is not a fair score (the bands respond differently to leaves and soil), so alignment is judged by how well their edges coincide: rs.uav.edge_agreement correlates the Sobel edge maps and ignores the empty border a warp leaves behind. coregister uses the same score to validate its own answer: a solution with too few inliers, an implausibly large shift, or edges that agree less than before is rejected and the band is returned unwarped (info["accepted"] is then False).

Code 
def edges(a):
    """Gradient magnitude, rescaled to 0-1 (for overlays)."""
    gy, gx = np.gradient(np.nan_to_num(a))
    m = np.hypot(gx, gy)
    return np.clip(m / np.percentile(m, 99), 0, 1)

aligned, reg = rs.uav.coregister(nir, green, method="sift", refine=True)
print({k: (round(v, 3) if isinstance(v, float) else v) for k, v in reg.items() if k != "H"})

Below, the three methods are compared on three pairs per crop with validation switched off, to see what each would do on its own, and then with validation on.

Code 
rows = []
for crop, d in pairs.groupby("crop"):
    for _, rr in d.sample(3, random_state=1).iterrows():

        g, n = p.sel(band="green").values, p.sel(band="nir").values

What the table shows on these crops:

  • SIFT is the most dependable: it improves edge agreement on nearly every pair.
  • ORB usually works but occasionally returns a wild homography from a handful of inliers.
  • ECC alone often fails to converge or drifts, because NIR and green look different; use it only as a refinement step after feature matching.
  • On some wheat frames every method agrees on a large shift that makes things worse: the regular drill rows look alike, so all three lock onto a neighbouring row. Validation catches this and keeps the unwarped band.

Lentil-Wheat frames have the sparsest canopy and fewest features, so always check inliers and accepted before trusting a registration.

NDVI, GNDVI and SAVI

With the registered pair, rs.uav.vegetation_indices adds the NIR indices automatically:

  • NDVI = \((NIR - R)/(NIR + R)\)
  • GNDVI = \((NIR - G)/(NIR + G)\), more sensitive to chlorophyll in dense canopies
  • SAVI = \(1.5\,(NIR - R)/(NIR + R + 0.5)\), damps the soil background in sparse canopies

Relative vs calibrated reflectance

The NIR and RGB cameras have different sensitivities, exposure settings and gains. A DN of 150 in NIR is not comparable to 150 in red, so DN-based NDVI is shifted and scaled by an unknown amount, and it shifts again whenever exposure or sunlight changes. Look at the distribution of NDVI medians per Drybean frame: in some sessions most pixels are negative, which real vegetation reflectance would never produce.

Code 
bean = pairs[pairs.crop == "drybean"].groupby("session").sample(4, random_state=0)
meds = []
for _, rr in bean.iterrows():

    meds.append({"session": rr.session, "nir_mean_DN": float(p.sel(band="nir").mean()) * 255,
                 "red_mean_DN": float(p.sel(band="red").mean()) * 255,

Calibration converts DN to reflectance. The cell below builds a synthetic example on top of a real pair: the pair is treated as “true” reflectance (scaled to realistic levels), a camera with a different gain and dark offset per band turns it into DN, and two reflectance panels (a 50 % grey and a 5 % black, as on commercial calibration targets) are pasted into the scene. Calibration then has to recover the truth.

Code 
true = pair.copy() * xr.DataArray([0.25, 0.30, 0.20, 0.55], dims="band", coords={"band": pair.band})  # reflectance scale
true = true.clip(0, 1)
panels = {"grey": ((20, 70, 20, 70), 0.50), "black": ((20, 70, 90, 140), 0.05)}  # (row0, row1, col0, col1), reflectance
for (r0, r1, c0, c1), rho in panels.values():
    true[:, r0:r1, c0:c1] = rho

gain = {"red": 380.0, "green": 300.0, "blue": 420.0, "nir": 210.0}  # DN per unit reflectance
dark = {"red": 12.0, "green": 10.0, "blue": 15.0, "nir": 22.0}      # dark offset (DN)
dn = xr.Dataset({b: (true.sel(band=b, drop=True) * gain[b] + dark[b]).clip(0, 255) for b in gain})
truth = xr.Dataset({b: true.sel(band=b, drop=True) for b in gain})

grey_dn = rs.uav.panel_dn_from_image(dn, panels["grey"][0])
black_dn = rs.uav.panel_dn_from_image(dn, panels["black"][0])
one_panel = rs.uav.panel_calibration(dn, grey_dn, {b: 0.50 for b in gain})

Raw-DN NDVI is biased because NIR and red have different gains and offsets. One panel fixes the gain but not the dark offset; two panels (empirical line) recover the truth. Real panels must be imaged at the flight’s altitude and sun angle, and be large enough to cover many pixels free of their edges.

Changing light. If clouds dim the sun by 30 % halfway through a flight, every DN drops. A downwelling light sensor (DLS) on top of the drone records the irradiance per band, and rs.uav.sunshine_normalize rescales each image to a reference irradiance:

Summary and exercises

Key points

  • A two-camera rig does not share one footprint. Register NIR to RGB (coregister) before computing an index, and check the fit with edge agreement.
  • NDVI, GNDVI and SAVI need NIR. On raw digital numbers they stay relative until a calibration converts them.
  • A panel, an empirical line and a sunshine correction are the three steps from DN toward reflectance.
  • Follow NDVI by crop and date only after the pairs that failed registration have been dropped.

GeoLattice functions used: rs.uav.load_pair, rs.uav.coregister, rs.uav.estimate_homography, rs.uav.edge_agreement, rs.uav.vegetation_indices, rs.uav.panel_calibration, rs.uav.panel_dn_from_image, rs.uav.empirical_line, rs.uav.sunshine_normalize, rs.uav.summarize_dates.

Exercises

  1. Register 20 Lentil-Wheat pairs with ORB and with SIFT. How often does validation reject each one, and for which reason?
  2. Add a third panel (20 %) to the synthetic example. Does the empirical line still give r2 close to 1? What happens if the black panel is partly in shadow (add 15 DN)?
  3. Replace NDVI with SAVI in Section 6. Does the soil-adjusted index change the early-season Drybean values more than the late ones?
  4. Compute per-session NIR and red DN means and plot them against NDVI. How much of the NDVI change between dates follows exposure?
