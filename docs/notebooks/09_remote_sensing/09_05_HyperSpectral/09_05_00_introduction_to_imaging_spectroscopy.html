Introduction to Imaging Spectroscopy

A multispectral sensor such as Landsat or Sentinel-2 records a handful of broad bands. An imaging spectrometer records hundreds of narrow, contiguous bands, so every pixel carries a near-continuous spectrum from the visible to the shortwave infrared. That spectrum holds the absorption features of pigments, water, minerals and gases, which is why hyperspectral data can tell kaolinite from calcite, estimate leaf chlorophyll or map a methane plume. This notebook introduces the physics, the sensors and the data products, then opens the chapter data with geolattice.rsense.hyperspectral: nine Planet Tanager scenes, a laboratory cube of white powders and the OHS and Pavia benchmark images used later in the chapter.

Twelve notebooks take a cube from the sensor file to a map.

  • Introduction to Imaging Spectroscopy. This notebook. Narrow bands and the sensors, then the chapter data: nine Planet Tanager scenes, a laboratory powder cube, and the OHS and Pavia benchmarks.
  • Reading and Exploring Hyperspectral Data. Read HDF5, NetCDF, ENVI, band GeoTIFFs and PNG folders into one GeoLattice cube, and georeference swath and OHS scenes.
  • Preprocessing Hyperspectral Cubes. Drop bad bands, mask clouds, turn radiance into reflectance, and smooth, differentiate or continuum-remove spectra.
  • Dimensionality Reduction and Band Selection. Compress Tanager and Pavia with PCA, MNF and ICA, and denoise by keeping the signal components.
  • Narrowband Indices and Vegetation Analysis. Target chlorophyll, the red edge, carotenoids, leaf water, cellulose and lignin with the narrowband index catalogue.
  • Biophysical and Biochemical Trait Retrieval. Simulate canopies with PROSAIL and retrieve LAI, chlorophyll and water with partial least squares, random forest and Gaussian processes.
  • Spectral Libraries and Spectral Matching. Build spectral libraries and compare spectra by spectral angle, information divergence, correlation and Euclidean distance.
  • Endmember Extraction and Spectral Unmixing. Extract endmembers and estimate mixture fractions, first on a synthetic scene with known answers.
  • Target and Anomaly Detection. Find a known material, or any unusual pixel, against a statistical model of the background.
  • Greenhouse Gas Plume Detection. Build a methane absorption spectrum from HITRAN and map enhancement over oil fields.
  • Thematic Applications Across Tanager Scenes. Burn severity at Malibu, snow and ice on Baffin Island, coastal water off Venezuela, and iron oxides in Bosnia and Herzegovina.
  • Deep Learning for Hyperspectral Classification. Train a 1D CNN, a 3D CNN, HybridSN and a spectral transformer on Pavia Centre, against a random forest and spectral angle.

Overview

What imaging spectroscopy measures

Light reflected from a surface carries the fingerprint of what it met. Chlorophyll absorbs blue and red light, leaf water absorbs near 970, 1200, 1450 and 1940 nm, clays absorb near 2200 nm, carbonates near 2340 nm and iron oxides near 900 nm. Most of these features are 10 to 50 nm wide. A broadband sensor averages them away; a spectrometer with 5 to 10 nm bands resolves them.

Imaging spectroscopy (also called hyperspectral imaging) measures that spectrum for every pixel of an image. The term was coined for NASA’s Airborne Imaging Spectrometer in the early 1980s, and AVIRIS (1987) set the template still in use: about 224 contiguous bands at 10 nm from 400 to 2500 nm.

The key ideas are:

  • Spectral range: the wavelengths covered. VNIR is about 400 to 1000 nm, SWIR about 1000 to 2500 nm. VNIR-only sensors (DESIS, OHS, PACE OCI) see pigments and water colour; VSWIR sensors (EMIT, EnMAP, PRISMA, Tanager) add the minerals, leaf water, cellulose and greenhouse gases.
  • Spectral sampling: the spacing between band centres, for example 5 nm.
  • Spectral resolution: the width of each band’s spectral response function, usually given as the full width at half maximum (FWHM). Sampling a little finer than the FWHM (Nyquist) lets the spectrum be reconstructed without gaps.
  • Signal to noise ratio (SNR): narrow bands collect few photons, so noise matters much more than in multispectral data. SNR is lowest at the ends of the range and inside absorption bands.

Panchromatic 1 300 to 400 nm Landsat 8 band 8

The hyperspectral data cube

Stacking the band images gives a three dimensional cube: two spatial axes and one spectral axis. A Tanager scene is about 426 bands by 700 rows by 800 columns, close to 1 GB as 32 bit floats. Each pixel is a spectrum; each band is an image. Three storage layouts are common: band sequential (BSQ, one image after another), band interleaved by line (BIL) and band interleaved by pixel (BIP). Products arrive as ENVI binary plus header, HDF5 or HDF-EOS5, NetCDF or multiband GeoTIFF.

How imaging spectrometers are built

  • Whiskbroom scanners sweep a mirror across track and disperse one pixel at a time onto a line detector (AVIRIS Classic, HyMap). Simple optics, but moving parts and short dwell time.
  • Pushbroom spectrometers image a whole line across track through a slit; a grating or prism spreads it over a 2-D detector (space by wavelength), and the platform’s motion builds the image line by line. Almost all modern sensors (EMIT, EnMAP, PRISMA, Tanager, AVIRIS-NG, HySpex, Specim) are pushbroom. Each detector column has its own response, which causes striping.
  • Snapshot and tunable filter cameras capture all bands at once or step through filters; they are common in laboratories, microscopes and drones. Filter based satellites such as OHS sit between multispectral and hyperspectral.

Silicon detectors cover the VNIR; mercury cadmium telluride (HgCdTe) detectors, cooled to below about 150 K, cover the SWIR. Two instrument artefacts deserve a name: spectral smile (the band centres shift slightly across the swath) and keystone (a pixel’s footprint shifts slightly with wavelength).

From radiance to reflectance

The sensor measures at-sensor radiance (W m⁻² sr⁻¹ µm⁻¹): sunlight that crossed the atmosphere twice and bounced off the surface, plus light scattered by air and aerosols. To compare pixels, dates and sensors it must be converted to surface reflectance, the fraction of incident light the surface reflects (0 to 1). Atmospheric correction codes such as ISOFIT, ATCOR, FLAASH and the mission processors invert a radiative transfer model per pixel, retrieving water vapour and aerosols on the way.

The atmosphere also leaves its own absorption bands, which mark where surface information is weak or absent:

Feature Wavelength (nm) Cause
Oxygen A band 760 O₂
Water vapour 940, 1140 H₂O
Strong water vapour (opaque) 1350 to 1450, 1800 to 1950 H₂O
Carbon dioxide 2010, 2060 CO₂
Methane 2200 to 2400 (strongest near 2300) CH₄

The opaque water bands are usually dropped as bad bands. The gas bands, on the other hand, are a signal in their own right: the methane notebook later in this chapter uses them.

Applications

  • Vegetation: chlorophyll, carotenoids, anthocyanins, leaf water, nitrogen, lignin and cellulose, plant stress and species mapping; LAI and canopy chlorophyll from radiative transfer models such as PROSAIL.
  • Geology and soils: mineral maps from diagnostic absorption features (clays, micas, carbonates, iron oxides), soil organic carbon and texture, mine waste monitoring.
  • Water: chlorophyll a, phycocyanin (cyanobacteria), suspended sediment, coloured dissolved organic matter, shallow water bathymetry and benthic habitats.
  • Snow and ice: grain size, liquid water, light absorbing impurities (dust, algae, soot).
  • Greenhouse gases: methane and carbon dioxide plumes from oil and gas, coal, landfills and livestock, with source emission rates.
  • Fire: active fire temperature, burn severity, post fire recovery.
  • Urban and industry: roofing and paving materials, plastics, impervious surfaces.
  • Laboratory and industry: food quality and contamination, sorting, pharmaceuticals, art conservation and forensics.

Hyperspectral Imaging with GeoLattice

geolattice.rsense.hyperspectral (imported here as hs) holds every function the chapter uses. Cubes are xarray.DataArray objects with dimensions (band, y, x) and band coordinates wavelength (nm), fwhm (nm) and good (bool). Swath products that are not yet on a map grid use (band, line, sample) in the same way. Readers exist for ENVI, EMIT, EnMAP, PRISMA, Tanager, OHS, AVIRIS and band described GeoTIFFs, and every analysis function (preprocessing, dimensionality reduction, indices, libraries, unmixing, detection, traits) accepts and returns this layout.

Why narrow bands matter

hs.synthetic_library returns idealised spectra of six materials built from Gaussian absorption features at 1 nm steps. They are teaching spectra, not measurements, but their features sit at the right wavelengths. Below they are convolved with the band responses of Tanager (426 bands, about 5.5 nm FWHM), Landsat 8/9 OLI and Sentinel-2 MSI with hs.resample_spectral, which applies a Gaussian response function for each target band.

def read_png_cube(folder):
    """Stack the *_<wavelength>nm.png band images of a HACARUS scene into a cube."""
    files = sorted(Path(folder).glob("*nm.png"), key=lambda p: float(re.search(r"_(\d+)nm", p.name)[1]))
    wl = [float(re.search(r"_(\d+)nm", p.name)[1]) for p in files]  # wavelength from each file name
    stack = np.stack([np.asarray(Image.open(p), dtype="float32") for p in files])  # (band, row, col)
    return hs.as_cube(stack, wl, name="dn")  # cube with a wavelength coordinate

It still shows the expected pattern: highest on the NIR plateau (about 25 near 730 to 750 nm), low wherever the scene is dark (the red chlorophyll absorption near 680 nm, the edges of the water bands near 1440 and 1970 nm) and lowest beyond 2480 nm, where few photons arrive.

Spectral signatures across Tanager scenes

Each scene contributes one material. A pixel is chosen by a simple rule on a few bands (read with bands= so only seven of the 426 bands are loaded), then the full spectrum is read as the mean of a 3 by 3 pixel window around it.

Code 
probe_idx = [hs.band_index(probe, w) for w in (560, 650, 860, 1650, 2200)]  # green, red, NIR, SWIR1, SWIR2


def nd(a, b):
    """Normalised difference of two band arrays."""
    return (a - b) / (a + b)


def closest(score, q):
    """(row, col) of the valid pixel whose score is nearest to its q-th quantile."""
    target = np.nanquantile(score, q)
    return np.unravel_index(np.nanargmin(np.abs(score - target)), score.shape)


rules = {  # material: (scene folder prefix, rule returning (row, col))
    "green vegetation": ("agriculture", lambda G, R, N, S1, S2: closest(nd(N, R), 0.995)),
    "bare soil (Permian Basin)": ("energy-mining",
                                  lambda G, R, N, S1, S2: closest(np.where(nd(N, R) < 0.12, S1, np.nan), 0.5)),

    "snow": ("snow-ice", lambda G, R, N, S1, S2: closest(np.where(nd(G, S1) > 0.8, G, np.nan), 0.5)),
    "urban (roofs and roads)": ("urban",

Each class has its own shape:

  • Green vegetation has the red edge and the highest NIR to red contrast (NDVI near 0.9).
  • Bare soil and desert rise steeply through the visible (iron oxides make them red), level off in the SWIR and decline beyond 2200 nm, where clays and carbonates absorb. The Syrian desert is the brightest.
  • Sea water is dark everywhere and close to zero beyond 700 nm.
  • Snow is very bright in the visible (0.9 to 1.1; values slightly above 1 come from the viewing geometry and the correction), has ice absorptions near 1030 and 1260 nm and falls to a few percent beyond 1500 nm. Its NDSI is the highest of all classes.
  • Urban surfaces are flat and moderately bright, a mixture of roofs, asphalt and concrete.
  • Burn scar pixels are dark in the NIR and relatively bright near 2200 nm, so their burn ratio (NBR) is the lowest.

The rules picked typical pixels, not pure ones: at 30 m most pixels mix several materials, and the urban spectrum in particular is an average of roofs, roads and trees. Spectral matching, unmixing and detection later in the chapter work with exactly these differences in shape.

Summary and exercises

Key points

  • Imaging spectrometers record hundreds of narrow contiguous bands; FWHM, sampling and SNR decide which absorption features can be resolved. Broad multispectral bands average away features such as the 2165 and 2205 nm clay doublet.
  • A hyperspectral cube is (band, y, x) with a wavelength axis; in GeoLattice every function works on that layout and selects bands by wavelength (band_at, band_index, sel_range).
  • Sensors measure radiance; surface reflectance requires atmospheric correction. Oxygen, water vapour and CO₂ leave absorption bands, and the opaque water vapour windows are flagged as bad bands.
  • hs.read_tanager reads Tanager ortho and basic products with subsetting by window, bbox, bands or wavelength; hs.read_tanager_ancillary returns the masks, water vapour, aerosol and geometry.
  • Vegetation, soil, water, snow, urban surfaces and burn scars have distinct spectral shapes, the basis of every method in this chapter.

geolattice.rsense.hyperspectral functions used: synthetic_library, as_cube, resample_spectral, wavelengths, band_index, band_at, sel_range, mean_spectrum, read_tanager, read_tanager_ancillary, read_ohs, plot_rgb, band_stats, plot_band_stats, spectral_viewer, wavelength_slider, plus the tables preprocess.SENSOR_SRF and preprocess.WATER_VAPOUR_RANGES.
