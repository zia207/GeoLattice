Thematic Applications Across Tanager Scenes

The previous notebooks built a toolbox; this one applies it to four landscapes from the Tanager sample collection, each with its own spectral question. A burn scar on the Malibu coast is mapped and measured with burn indices and char spectra; snow, sea ice and open water on Baffin Island are separated and the 1030 nm ice absorption is tested as a grain size proxy; turbid coastal water off Venezuela is corrected for sun glint and mapped for sediment and chlorophyll; and iron oxide rich ground in Bosnia and Herzegovina is found with an index and checked with its spectrum.

Overview

Fire and burn severity

Fire removes green leaves (NIR falls), exposes ash, char and soil and dries what remains (SWIR rises). The normalised burn ratio, NBR = (R860 − R2200) / (R860 + R2200), falls from about 0.4 to 0.7 over healthy vegetation to near zero or below over severe burns. With images before and after a fire the difference (dNBR) measures severity; with one post fire image the NBR and the spectra of char and ash still outline the burn. Hyperspectral sensors also see active fires through thermal emission in the SWIR and the potassium emission lines at 766 and 770 nm.

Snow and ice

Snow is the brightest natural surface in the visible and one of the darkest in the SWIR, because ice absorbs strongly beyond about 1400 nm. The normalised difference snow index, NDSI = (R560 − R1650) / (R560 + R1650), separates snow from clouds and other surfaces. Weaker ice absorptions at 1030 and 1260 nm deepen as grain size grows, because light travels farther through ice in larger grains; the depth or area of the 1030 nm feature is a classic grain size proxy (Nolin and Dozier 2000). Impurities (dust, soot, algae) lower the visible reflectance.

Coastal and inland water

Water leaving radiance is a few percent of the total signal, so atmospheric and sun glint correction matter. A common glint correction subtracts the NIR or SWIR reflectance, where water leaving signal is negligible, from every band. Suspended sediment raises red and NIR reflectance (roughly linearly up to tens of g m⁻³); chlorophyll a creates a dip near 670 nm and a peak near 700 nm, which the normalised difference chlorophyll index, NDCI = (R708 − R665) / (R708 + R665), exploits in turbid water (Mishra and Mishra 2012); cyanobacteria add a phycocyanin dip near 620 nm.

Iron oxides and soils

Ferric iron (hematite, goethite) absorbs strongly in the blue and green and weakly near 900 nm, which makes iron rich soils and rocks red or orange. A red to green ratio, or the depth of the 900 nm feature, maps them; ferrous iron (in many silicates) absorbs near 1000 nm.

Thematic Mapping with GeoLattice

Everything here uses functions from earlier notebooks: hs.read_tanager (with bands= to read only what each step needs), hs.index (NBR_hs, NDCI, FerricIron), hs.band_depth, hs.quality_mask, hs.classify_spectral and hs.spectra_at style pixel picks.

Learning objectives

  1. Map a burn scar and compare the spectra of burned and unburned land.
  2. Separate snow, sea ice and open water and test the 1030 nm ice feature as a grain size proxy.
  3. Correct water reflectance for sun glint and map turbidity and chlorophyll.
  4. Find iron oxide rich surfaces with an index and confirm them by their spectra.

Setup

Code 
import warnings  # warning control
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")  # hide routine library warnings

from pathlib import Path  # file paths

import numpy as np  # arrays
import pandas as pd  # tables
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt  # figures

import geolattice  # GeoLattice
from geolattice.rsense import hyperspectral as hs  # hyperspectral cubes, readers and tools

plt.rcParams.update({"figure.dpi": 110})  # figure resolution
DATA = Path("../../../../Data/08_remote_sensing/08_05_HyperSpectral")  # data folder next to this notebook
TANAGER = DATA / "Tanger"  # Planet Tanager sample scenes
OUT = Path("outputs")  # results written by this notebook
OUT.mkdir(exist_ok=True)  # create it if needed


def tanager_file(category, product="sr"):
    """Path of a Tanager product (sr or radiance) for a scene category folder."""
    return next((TANAGER / category).glob(f"*/*_ortho_{product}_hdf5.h5"))


print("GeoLattice", geolattice.__version__)  # library version used for this notebook

from matplotlib.colors import ListedColormap  # class colours
from matplotlib.patches import Patch  # legend entries


def bands_near(path, wavelengths_nm):
    """Indices of the Tanager bands nearest to the given wavelengths."""
    probe = hs.read_tanager(path, window=(slice(0, 1), slice(0, 1)))
    return sorted({hs.band_index(probe, w) for w in wavelengths_nm})


def pick(score, mask, q):
    """(row, col) of the pixel in mask whose score is nearest its q-th quantile."""
    s = np.where(mask, score, np.nan)
    return np.unravel_index(np.nanargmin(np.abs(s - np.nanquantile(s, q))), s.shape)


def mean3(cube, rc):
    """Mean 3 x 3 spectrum around (row, col)."""
    r, c = rc
    return cube.values[:, max(r - 1, 0):r + 2, max(c - 1, 0):c + 2].reshape(cube.sizes["band"], -1).mean(1)

A burn scar on the Malibu coast

The Franklin Fire started on 9 December 2024 in the Santa Monica Mountains above Malibu. Six days later Tanager imaged the area. Burned land is found with NBR over the land part of the scene.

Code 
fire_f = tanager_file("fire")
fire = hs.read_tanager(fire_f, bands=bands_near(fire_f, [461, 561, 651, 766, 770, 861, 1650, 2200]))  # 8 bands, whole scene
nbr = hs.index(fire, "NBR_hs")  # (R860 - R2200) / (R860 + R2200)
R, G, N, S2 = (hs.band_at(fire, w).values for w in (651, 561, 861, 2200))
land = ((G - N) / (G + N) < 0) & np.isfinite(N)  # not water
burn = land & (nbr.values < 0.05)  # low NBR on land: burned (or bare) ground
px_area = 30 * 30 / 1e6  # km2 per pixel
print(f"land {land.sum() * px_area:.0f} km2 | NBR < 0.05: {burn.sum() * px_area:.1f} km2 | "
      f"NBR on land: median {np.nanmedian(nbr.values[land]):.2f}")
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(17, 5.2))
hs.plot_rgb(fire, (651, 561, 461), ax=axes[0], title="True colour")
hs.plot_rgb(fire, (2200, 861, 651), ax=axes[1], title="SWIR composite (2200, 860, 650 nm)")
im = axes[2].imshow(np.where(land, nbr, np.nan), cmap="RdYlGn", vmin=-0.3, vmax=0.6)
axes[2].contour(burn, [0.5], colors="k", linewidths=0.4); axes[2].set_title("NBR on land, NBR < 0.05 outlined"); axes[2].axis("off")
plt.colorbar(im, ax=axes[2], shrink=0.7)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
land 146 km2 | NBR < 0.05: 23.4 km2 | NBR on land: median 0.29

full = hs.read_tanager(fire_f, window=(slice(0, 400), slice(None)))  # all bands, northern (land) part of the scene
full = hs.drop_bad_bands(full)
nb_full = hs.index(full, "NBR_hs").values
lnd = land[:400]
picks = {"burned (lowest NBR)": pick(nb_full, lnd, 0.01), "low NBR, bright (dry grass or partly burned)": pick(nb_full, lnd, 0.15),
         "unburned chaparral": pick(nb_full, lnd, 0.9), "bare or urban": pick(np.where(nb_full < 0.15, hs.band_at(full, 650).values, np.nan), lnd, 0.98)}
wl = hs.wavelengths(full)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(13, 4.5))
for name, rc in picks.items():
    s = mean3(full, rc)
    ax.plot(wl, s, lw=1.3, label=f"{name}, NBR {nb_full[rc]:.2f}")
ax.set(xlabel="Wavelength (nm)", ylabel="Reflectance", title="Spectra across the fire scene"); ax.legend(fontsize=8)
plt.show()
print(f"maximum SWIR reflectance on land at 2200 nm: {np.nanmax(S2[land]):.2f} (flames would stand far above any surface)")
maximum SWIR reflectance on land at 2200 nm: 0.47 (flames would stand far above any surface)

Low NBR outlines a compact burn scar in the mountains, which the SWIR composite shows in magenta against the green of unburned chaparral. The 23 km² below the threshold include more than the scar: scattered bare, built up and dry pixels along the coast also fall below 0.05, so the scar itself is smaller. The spectra explain the index. The burned pixel has lost the red edge and NIR plateau of vegetation and is dark and nearly flat, rising only slowly into the SWIR (char and ash), while unburned chaparral keeps a clear red edge. Pixels just below the threshold can still look like dry vegetation (bright, with a weak red edge), and the “bare or urban” spectrum is twice as bright as char in the visible: brightness and shape separate them from char, which a single NBR threshold does not. No pixel on land has SWIR reflectance above about 0.5, so there are no active flames six days after ignition. With an image from before the fire, dNBR would separate burned land from naturally bare ground.

Snow, sea ice and grain size on Baffin Island

Code 
snow_f = tanager_file("snow-ice")
sn = hs.read_tanager(snow_f, wavelength_range=(400, 1700))  # VNIR and the start of the SWIR, whole scene
sn = sn.isel(band=np.flatnonzero(sn["good"].values))
G, N, S1 = (hs.band_at(sn, w).values for w in (560, 860, 1650))
ndsi = (G - S1) / (G + S1)
valid = np.isfinite(G)
cls = np.full(G.shape, np.nan)
cls[valid & (ndsi > 0.4) & (G > 0.5)] = 0  # snow and snow covered ice: bright, high NDSI
cls[valid & (ndsi > 0.4) & (G <= 0.5) & (G > 0.12)] = 1  # darker ice: bare, wet or thin snow
cls[valid & (G <= 0.12) & (N < 0.05)] = 2  # open water: dark, NIR near zero
cls[valid & np.isnan(cls)] = 3  # other: rock, cloud shadow, mixed
names = ["bright snow", "darker ice or wet snow", "open water", "other"]
frac = pd.Series({n: float(np.mean(cls[valid] == i)) for i, n in enumerate(names)})
cmap = ListedColormap(["#f7fbff", "#9ecae1", "#08306b", "#bdbdbd"])
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(17, 5.5))
hs.plot_rgb(sn, (651, 561, 461), ax=axes[0], title="True colour")
im = axes[1].imshow(ndsi, cmap="RdBu", vmin=-0.5, vmax=1); axes[1].set_title("NDSI"); axes[1].axis("off"); plt.colorbar(im, ax=axes[1], shrink=0.7)
axes[2].imshow(cls, cmap=cmap, vmin=-0.5, vmax=3.5, interpolation="nearest"); axes[2].axis("off"); axes[2].set_title("Classes")
axes[2].legend(handles=[Patch(facecolor=c, edgecolor="k", label=n) for c, n in zip(cmap.colors, names)], fontsize=8, loc="lower left")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
frac.round(3).to_frame("fraction of the scene")

Code 
bd = hs.band_depth(hs.smooth(sn, window=5), 1030, 950, 1100)  # ice absorption near 1030 nm
depth = np.where(cls == 0, bd["depth"].values, np.nan)  # bright snow only
qs = {f"{int(q * 100)}th percentile depth": pick(depth, cls == 0, q) for q in (0.05, 0.5, 0.95)}
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(15, 4.8), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1, 1.3]})
im = axes[0].imshow(depth, cmap="viridis", vmin=np.nanpercentile(depth, 2), vmax=np.nanpercentile(depth, 98))
axes[0].set_title("1030 nm band depth over bright snow"); axes[0].axis("off"); plt.colorbar(im, ax=axes[0], shrink=0.8)
w = hs.wavelengths(sn)
for name, rc in qs.items():
    axes[1].plot(w, mean3(sn, rc), lw=1.3, label=f"{name} ({bd['depth'].values[rc]:.3f})")
axes[1].set(xlabel="Wavelength (nm)", ylabel="Reflectance", title="Snow spectra by 1030 nm depth (larger depth: larger grains)")
axes[1].legend(fontsize=8)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
print(f"1030 nm depth over bright snow: median {np.nanmedian(depth):.3f}, 5 to 95 percent {np.nanpercentile(depth, 5):.3f} to {np.nanpercentile(depth, 95):.3f}")
1030 nm depth over bright snow: median 0.136, 5 to 95 percent 0.100 to 0.144

The scene is 94 percent bright snow over land and sea ice, with about 3 percent dark open water and 2 percent darker ice. NDSI is high over all snow and ice and low over water. The small bright patch classed as “other” has low NDSI although it is bright: a cloud, which unlike snow reflects strongly at 1650 nm.

The 1030 nm depth over bright snow varies only between about 0.10 and 0.14, and its map is dominated by a smooth gradient across the scene and by topographic texture rather than by patches of snow type. The spectra show why: the median pixel is far brighter than the others (reflectance above 1 on a sunlit slope), so illumination and slope change the spectra more than grain size does. Band depth, a ratio to the local continuum, removes much of a brightness change but not the change in path length and diffuse light with slope and sun angle. Ranking grain size from this map would need a terrain illumination correction first, and converting depth to micrometres a snow radiative transfer model.

Turbid water off the Paraguaná Peninsula

The coastal scene shows clear Caribbean water, a refinery coast and a pale sediment plume. Water is masked by NDWI, glint is removed by subtracting each pixel’s mean reflectance between 1550 and 1650 nm (where water leaving signal is nil), and two water quality proxies are mapped: red reflectance at 650 nm for turbidity and NDCI for chlorophyll.

Code 
cw_f = tanager_file("coastal-water-bodies")
cw = hs.read_tanager(cw_f, wavelength_range=(400, 1700))
cw = cw.isel(band=np.flatnonzero(cw["good"].values))
G, N = hs.band_at(cw, 560).values, hs.band_at(cw, 860).values
water = ((G - N) / (G + N) > 0.2) & np.isfinite(G)  # water by NDWI

Over water the SWIR reflectance is small but not zero (about 0.017), and its pattern of wind streaks and wave fields is the sun glint that the correction removes; it is also higher next to the plume and the coast, where adjacency effects from the bright land and sediment add light. After correction the clearest water is blue with a reflectance of a few percent at 450 nm and almost nothing beyond 600 nm, typical of oligotrophic Caribbean water, while the plume raises the green, red and NIR strongly: suspended sediment scatters light at all wavelengths. The NDCI map is the cautionary part. In clear water both its bands are close to zero, so the index is dominated by noise and by the detector stripes; it only becomes meaningful where the water is productive or turbid. Over all water NDCI and red reflectance are weakly negatively correlated (r about -0.34), and in the plume the 708 nm reflectance is set by sediment scattering, not by chlorophyll; separating the two requires bio-optical models or field samples. Red reflectance alone is a robust relative turbidity map; converting it to g m⁻³ needs a calibration such as Nechad et al. (2010).

Iron oxides in Bosnia and Herzegovina

The winter scene of the natural lands collection shows dormant forest and fields and a conspicuous red brown patch. The ferric iron index (R750 / R560) finds such surfaces; the spectra confirm what they are.

Summary and exercises

Key points

  • Burn scars show low NBR and dark, flat char spectra; brightness and shape separate them from bare ground and dry vegetation, and a pre fire image would give burn severity.
  • NDSI separates snow and ice from water, rock and cloud; the 1030 nm ice absorption is a grain size proxy, but on rugged terrain illumination must be corrected first.
  • Coastal water needs glint correction; red reflectance maps turbidity, while NDCI is noise in clear water and confounded by sediment in turbid water.
  • Iron oxide rich surfaces are found with a red to green ratio and confirmed by their 900 nm feature.
