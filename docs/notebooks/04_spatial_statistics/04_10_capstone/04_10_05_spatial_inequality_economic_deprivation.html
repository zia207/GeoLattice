Spatial Inequality and Economic Deprivation in the United States

A county-level analysis of socioeconomic vulnerability, environmental exposure, and health disparities

This is Part 10 (Capstone) of the spatial-statistics module. It follows the course guideline spatial_inequality.qmd: build a county-level Economic Deprivation Index (EDI), test whether deprivation is spatially clustered, locate the high- and low-deprivation regions, and model both what goes with deprivation and what deprivation goes with.

All county indicators come from the local USA Health Data Atlas SQLite database (Data/00_shared/data_spatial_stat/usa_health_atlas_database/): County Health Rankings (CHR), CDC/ATSDR Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), and Census PEP demography. Geometry is US_COUNTY.shp (CONUS counties, NAD83 Albers).

Economic deprivation is the primary construct here. Health and environmental indicators are used to describe its geographic consequences, which is the reverse emphasis of the health-disparities capstone (04_10_01).

Overview

How are economic deprivation and socioeconomic disadvantage spatially distributed across U.S. counties, and how are they associated with health outcomes, environmental exposures, and healthcare access?

Objectives

Objective Question Sections
1. Measure economic deprivation Can a transparent county EDI be built from Atlas indicators, and is it robust to how it is weighted? 3
2. Identify spatial inequality Is deprivation clustered, where are the hot and cold spots, and how much of the inequality lies between states and regions? 5 to 7
3. Explain geographic disparities What demographic and regional characteristics go with deprivation (Model 1), and how is deprivation associated with health (Model 2)? 8 to 12

Hypotheses

Hypothesis Statistical test
H1: Economic deprivation is spatially clustered across U.S. counties. Global Moran’s I
H2: High-deprivation counties form statistically significant geographic hotspots. LISA (FDR) and Getis-Ord Gi*
H3: Economic deprivation is positively associated with adverse health outcomes. OLS, SAR, SEM, SDM with impacts
H4: The deprivation-health relationship varies geographically. GW correlation, GWR, MGWR
H5: Environmental burdens are disproportionately concentrated in economically deprived counties. Bivariate LISA and regression

The EDI is built from CHR measures only. The SVI socioeconomic theme is kept out of the index so that it can serve as an independent check, and no EDI component is used again as a predictor in a model that has the EDI on either side.

Workflow (guideline step in brackets)

Step What we do
Data [1, 2] SQL extract from the Atlas database; documented reference years
Cleaning [3] FIPS, duplicates, missingness by variable and state, scale and outlier checks, geometry join

Deprivation and health [6] Bivariate LISA: EDI against diabetes, premature death, and PM2.5
Model 1 [guideline Section 5] What goes with deprivation: OLS, LM tests, SAR, SEM
Model 2 [7] Deprivation and health: OLS, SAR, SEM, SDM; direct, indirect, and total impacts; four outcomes

Machine learning [9] Linear, Ridge, Elastic Net, Random Forest, XGBoost; random versus state-blocked CV; TreeSHAP
Report [10] Hypothesis summary, decision log, limitations

Caveats. County associations are ecological, not individual causal effects. CHR measures are release-year values, several of them model-based, and do not all refer to the same data year. The Atlas database does not hold median income, PLACES, CDC WONDER mortality, overdose, cancer, or soil layers at the time of writing, so those parts of the guideline are listed as extensions. The county shapefile covers the contiguous United States, so Alaska and Hawaii are not in the study region. Every number below is computed in this notebook.

Cleaning and integration

The guideline’s Step 3 checklist: five-character FIPS, duplicate county-year records, missingness by variable and by state, consistent scales, implausible values, and the join to county geometry. Outcomes are never imputed. Counties with a missing model input are dropped (complete cases), and the cell reports how many and where.

Code 
# 1) FIPS format and duplicates
long["fips"] = long["fips"].astype(str).str.zfill(5)
assert long["fips"].str.fullmatch(r"\d{5}").all(), "FIPS codes must be five digits"
n_dup = int(long.duplicated(["fips", "dataset_name", "variable", "year"]).sum())
print(f"five-digit FIPS: OK  |  duplicate county-variable-year rows: {n_dup}")

wide = long.pivot_table(index="fips", columns=["dataset_name", "variable", "year"], values="value")
table = pd.DataFrame(index=wide.index)
for name, key in SPEC.items():
    table[name] = wide[key].to_numpy()

# 2) Consistent scales: CHR proportions -> percent; reverse-code attainment; derived change
for name, (_var, reverse) in EDI_SPEC.items():
    table[name] = 100 * (1 - table[name]) if reverse else 100 * table[name]
table["diabetes_pct"] = 100 * table["diabetes"]
table["uninsured_pct"] = 100 * table["uninsured"]
table["pop_change"] = 100 * (table["pop"] / table["pop_base"] - 1)

# 3) Join geometry (contiguous United States)
shp = gpd.read_file(SHP)
shp["fips"] = shp["FIPS"].astype(int).astype(str).str.zfill(5)
merged = shp.merge(table.reset_index(), on="fips", how="left")
merged["region"] = merged["STATE"].map(STATE_REGION)
assert merged["region"].notna().all(), "every state needs a Census region"

)

Constructing the Economic Deprivation Index

Each indicator is standardized, \(Z_{ij} = (X_{ij} - \bar X_j) / SD(X_j)\), after the two attainment measures have been reverse-coded so that higher always means more deprived. The equal-weighted index is the mean of the six z-scores:

\[EDI_i = \frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p} Z^{*}_{ij}\]

The guideline recommends computing a PCA-based index as well and comparing county rankings. Three further checks are added: dropping each component in turn, adding income inequality (which the guideline flags as context-dependent), and comparing the result with the SVI socioeconomic theme, which is built from different (ACS) inputs.

Is deprivation spatially clustered? (H1)

Global Moran’s I with row-standardized Queen contiguity and 999 permutations, as the guideline specifies. Counties left without a neighbor after the complete-case filter are linked to their nearest county. The statistic is computed for the EDI, each component, and the two alternative indices, and repeated with k-nearest-neighbor weights as a check.

Code 
def attach_islands(w, xy):
    # Link each island to its nearest non-island county, both ways, then row-standardise.
    ids = list(w.id_order)
    pos = {i: k for k, i in enumerate(ids)}
    islands = set(w.islands())
    neighbors = {i: list(w.neighbors[i]) for i in ids}
    if not islands:
        return W(neighbors, ids=ids, transform="row")
    tree = cKDTree(xy)
    for isl in islands:
        _, idxs = tree.query(xy[pos[isl]], k=min(25, len(ids)))
        for j in np.atleast_1d(idxs):
            other = ids[int(j)]
            if other == isl or other in islands:
                continue
            if other not in neighbors[isl]:
                neighbors[isl].append(other)
            if isl not in neighbors[other]:
                neighbors[other].append(isl)
            break
    return W(neighbors, ids=ids, transform="row")

How much of the inequality is between places?

Clustering maps do not say how large the geographic gaps are. Decomposing the county variance of the EDI into a between-group and a within-group part gives a direct measure: the share that would remain if every county took its state (or region) mean. A population-weighted version asks the same question for people instead of counties. Diabetes prevalence is decomposed the same way for comparison.

Code 
def between_share(values, groups, weights=None):
    # Share of (weighted) variance that lies between group means.
    v = np.asarray(values, float)
    wt = np.ones_like(v) if weights is None else np.asarray(weights, float)
    grand = np.average(v, weights=wt)
    total = np.average((v - grand) ** 2, weights=wt)
    frame = pd.DataFrame({"v": v, "w": wt, "g": np.asarray(groups)})
    gm = frame.groupby("g").apply(lambda d: np.average(d["v"], weights=d["w"]), include_groups=False)
    gw = frame.groupby("g")["w"].sum()
    return float(np.sum(gw * (gm - grand) ** 2) / gw.sum() / total)

Model 1: what goes with economic deprivation?

\[EDI_i = f(\text{demography, rurality, region})\]

The dependent variable is the EDI. Predictors are population change since 2010, age composition, rural share, racial and ethnic composition, and Census region (Northeast as the reference). Does the deprivation-health relationship vary by region? (H4)

Three local methods from the guideline are applied to the EDI and diabetes.

  • Geographically weighted correlation (GWCORR): the correlation between EDI and diabetes within each county’s neighborhood.
  • GWR: the Model 2 regression with coefficients that vary over space, using an adaptive bisquare kernel and a small grid of neighbor counts chosen by AICc.
  • MGWR: a separate bandwidth for each predictor, fitted with the PySAL mgwr package for five Deep South states (the search is slow for all counties). The cell is skipped if mgwr is not installed.

Local coefficients are less reliable where counties are large and sparse, so the West is read with more caution.

Machine learning as a complementary analysis

Five models predict diabetes prevalence from the EDI, demography, PM2.5, and the uninsured rate: linear regression, ridge, elastic net, random forest, and XGBoost. Each is evaluated twice: with ordinary random 5-fold cross-validation, and with folds that hold out whole states. The guideline’s warning is that random splits put neighboring, similar counties in both the training and the test set, so the gap between the two columns measures that optimism. TreeSHAP (from XGBoost’s pred_contribs) describes what drives the predictions. LightGBM, also named in the guideline, is left out to avoid another dependency.

Takeaways

  1. Measurement. A six-indicator EDI is internally consistent and robust: PCA weights, dropping any component, or adding income inequality barely change the county ranking, and the index agrees closely with the independently built SVI socioeconomic theme.
  2. Clustering (H1). Deprivation is strongly clustered under both Queen and KNN weights. Food insecurity is the most clustered component and college attainment the least.
  3. Hot spots (H2). After FDR adjustment the High-High counties are almost all in the South (the Texas border, the Mississippi Delta, eastern Kentucky, the Black Belt), and the Low-Low counties are in the upper Midwest and northern Plains.
  4. Between and within. States account for less than half of the county variance in deprivation. Most inequality is between counties of the same state.
  5. What goes with deprivation (Model 1). Population decline and the shares of Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, and American Indian residents are the strongest correlates. Once spatial dependence is modeled, the Census-region differences are no longer distinguishable from zero.
  6. Deprivation and health (H3). The EDI is associated with higher diabetes prevalence, more premature death, more poor mental health days, and lower life expectancy in every model. For diabetes the association is almost entirely with a county’s own deprivation; the neighboring-county (indirect) part is close to zero.
  7. Geographic variation (H4). The local EDI coefficient is positive everywhere but about twice as large in the Deep South as in the Midwest.
  8. Environmental burden (H5). PM2.5 is only weakly related to deprivation nationally. Deprived counties in the Southeast have high-PM2.5 surroundings, while deprived counties in the mountain West and northern Plains have low-PM2.5 surroundings.
  9. Prediction. Under state-blocked cross-validation the regularized linear models do as well as random forest and XGBoost, and random splits flatter the tree models most. Out-of-fold errors stay spatially autocorrelated for every model.

Limitations and policy reading

County-level associations are ecological: they describe places, not individuals, and do not establish that deprivation causes the health differences. Several CHR measures are model-based estimates, and food insecurity in particular is modeled partly from poverty and unemployment, which may inflate its correlation with the other components. The rural share is a 2010 Census value. Complete-case filtering removes mainly small counties and all of Connecticut, whose 2022 planning-region FIPS codes do not match the county shapefile. With those limits in mind, the maps point to two policy-relevant facts: deprivation and poor health are concentrated in the same contiguous multi-county areas of the South, which favors regional over county-by-county programs, and large within-state gaps mean that state averages hide many of the most deprived counties.
