Geostatistical Simulation

Kriging gives one map: the best estimate at every location. That map is deliberately smooth. Away from the samples it relaxes toward the mean, so the highs are too low, the lows are too high, and the map has less spatial variability than the ground it describes. For a question about a single location (“what is the most likely value here?”) that is the right answer. For a question about many locations at once (“how much of this field exceeds the threshold?”, “is there a connected path of high permeability?”, “how much ore is in this block?”) the smooth map gives a biased answer, and the kriging variance cannot fix it, because it describes one location at a time.

Geostatistical simulation answers those questions by drawing many maps instead of one. Each map, a realization, honours the data, has the histogram of the variable and its variogram, and is as rough as the real field. The realizations differ from one another away from the data, and that spread is the uncertainty. Every quantity you compute from a map (a volume, a probability, a flow, a cost) is computed on each realization in turn, which gives a distribution of answers instead of a single, smoothed one.

This notebook is the map for simulation in GeoLattice. It explains why kriging smooths, how simulation works, and what each method in geolattice.geostatistics.simulation does. It then runs every one of them on the same synthetic field, where the truth is known.

Ten notebooks draw many maps from one sample, so a volume, a threshold, or a connected path has a distribution.

What geostatistical simulation is

The smoothing effect of kriging

Write the variable as a random field \(Z(\mathbf{x})\) and the kriging estimate as \(Z^*(\mathbf{x})\). Kriging is unbiased and minimizes the error variance \(\sigma_K^2(\mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{Var}\bigl(Z(\mathbf{x}) - Z^*(\mathbf{x})\bigr)\). For simple kriging the error is orthogonal to the estimate, which gives

\[ \operatorname{Var}\bigl(Z^*(\mathbf{x})\bigr) = \sigma^2 - \sigma_K^2(\mathbf{x}). \]

The estimate has the full variance \(\sigma^2\) only at a sample, where \(\sigma_K^2 = 0\). Everywhere else it has less variance than the variable, and far from the data, where \(\sigma_K^2 \to \sigma^2\), it has almost none. That is the smoothing effect. It also changes the variogram of the map: the kriged surface is more continuous at short lags than the field it estimates.

Realizations

A conditional simulation draws fields \(z^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x})\), \(\ell = 1, \ldots, L\), from the distribution of \(Z\) given the data. Each realization:

  • honours the data: \(z^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{x}_i) = z(\mathbf{x}_i)\) at every sample (for a model without nugget);
  • reproduces the histogram of the variable (in expectation over realizations);
  • reproduces the variogram, so it has the right roughness.

The realizations differ away from the data. Averaged node by node they converge to the kriging estimate (the E-type map). Their spread at a node converges to the kriging variance. Their joint behaviour, which kriging cannot describe, is what simulation adds.

An unconditional simulation drops the first property: it reproduces the histogram and variogram but ignores the data. It is used as a building block (conditioning by kriging, below), and to study what a model implies before any data are collected.

Transfer functions

The reason to simulate is usually downstream. A transfer function is anything computed from a whole map: the area above a threshold, the volume of ore above a cutoff, a contaminant-transport model, a flow simulator, a cost. Running the transfer function on each realization gives a distribution of outcomes. Running it on the kriged map gives one outcome, and for a non-linear transfer function that outcome is biased. Extremes are muted, and connected features break up.

How simulation works

The workflow

Most simulation studies follow the same steps:

  1. Explore and decluster the data so the histogram represents the area, not the sampling pattern.
  2. Transform to a convenient space. Gaussian methods use the normal-score transform \(y = G^{-1}\bigl(F(z)\bigr)\), where \(F\) is the data cdf and \(G\) the standard normal cdf.
  3. Model the spatial structure. A variogram (two-point statistics) for most methods, indicator variograms for indicator and annealing methods, a truncation rule for plurigaussian simulation, a training image for multiple-point statistics.
  4. Simulate \(L\) realizations.
  5. Back-transform to original units.
  6. Check each realization: data honoured, histogram and variogram reproduced.
  7. Post-process: E-type and conditional variance maps, probabilities of exceeding a threshold, quantile maps, or a transfer function run on each realization.

The sequential principle

The joint distribution of the values at \(N\) grid nodes, given the data \((n)\), factorizes into a product of univariate conditionals:

\[ f(z_1, \ldots, z_N \mid (n)) = f(z_1 \mid (n))\, f(z_2 \mid (n), z_1) \cdots f(z_N \mid (n), z_1, \ldots, z_{N-1}). \]

Sequential simulation draws from that product one factor at a time. It visits the nodes along a random path, estimates the local conditional distribution from the data and the previously simulated nodes, draws a value, and adds it to the conditioning set. For a Gaussian field the local distribution is \(\mathcal{N}\bigl(y^*_{SK}, \sigma^2_{SK}\bigr)\), so each step is one simple-kriging system. Re-using the simulated nodes is what gives the realization the full variogram. In practice the search is limited to the nearest neighbours, which is an approximation.

Conditioning by kriging

A method that produces only unconditional fields can still be conditioned (Journel and Huijbregts, 1978). Simulate an unconditional field \(y_u\) at the data locations and the grid, krige the data and the simulated values at the data locations with the same weights, and combine:

\[ z_{cs}(\mathbf{x}) = z^*_K(\mathbf{x}) + \bigl[y_u(\mathbf{x}) - y^*_{u,K}(\mathbf{x})\bigr]. \]

The kriged data give the right local mean. The bracket is a simulated kriging error with the right variance and covariance. Turning bands and FFT simulation are conditioned this way in GeoLattice.

The simulation methods

Each module in geolattice.geostatistics.simulation implements one family. All of them are imported from geolattice.geostatistics.

Module Main call Variable How a realization is built Exact?
sgsim.py sgsim continuous Sequential Gaussian simulation, GSLIB style: octant search, simple or ordinary kriging, normal-score transform approximate (neighbourhood)
sgcosim.py GaussianCoSimulator, sgcosim, sgcosim_hierarchical, sgsim_gpu continuous (+ secondary) Sequential Gaussian simulation with precomputed neighbour lists, and co-simulation with a secondary variable by collocated cokriging (Markov model 1) approximate (neighbourhood)
sisim.py sisim continuous, by thresholds Sequential indicator simulation, GSLIB style: indicator kriging at each threshold, order-relation correction, tail models approximate
sicosim.py IndicatorCoSimulator, sicosim, SoftCalibration categorical or continuous (+ soft data) Sequential indicator simulation and co-simulation with soft secondary data (Markov-Bayes) approximate
lusim.py LUSimulator, lusim continuous Cholesky (LU) factor of the full covariance matrix; conditional by simple or ordinary kriging exact
tbsim.py TBSimulator, tbsim continuous Turning bands: a sum of 1-D line processes over many directions, conditioned by kriging exact covariance in expectation
fftsim.py FFTSimulator, fftsim continuous, regular grid FFT circulant embedding of the covariance, conditioned by kriging exact
direct_sequential.py DirectSequentialSimulator, dssim_direct continuous, original units Direct sequential simulation: kriging in original units, draws from the global histogram approximate
annealing.py SimulatedAnnealing, sasim continuous or categorical Simulated annealing: swap values until the image matches target variograms (and a correlation with a secondary) target-matching, not a probability model
pgs.py PluriGaussianSimulator, pgsim, TruncationRule categorical Plurigaussian: truncate one or two latent Gaussian fields with a rule that controls which facies may touch exact latent fields; Gibbs sampler for the data

Sequential methods

sgsim and GaussianCoSimulator simulate a Gaussian (normal-score) field along a random path. sisim and IndicatorCoSimulator replace the Gaussian conditional distribution with one estimated by indicator kriging, so each threshold or class can have its own continuity. DirectSequentialSimulator krigs in original units and draws from the global histogram. That keeps the original-unit variogram, which normal-score methods reproduce only after back-transformation, if at all. All of them are approximate because each node sees only its nearest neighbours.

Matrix and spectral methods

LUSimulator factorizes the covariance of all data and grid nodes once. Every realization is then a matrix-vector product, exact for any model, but the matrix is dense, so the method is limited to a few thousand locations. FFTSimulator embeds the grid covariance in a periodic torus whose eigenvalues come from one FFT, which makes it exact and fast on regular grids of millions of nodes. TBSimulator sums 1-D processes along many lines. It works on any set of points and scales linearly with their number; the covariance is exact on average over realizations.

Categorical methods

Indicator simulation gives each class its own indicator variogram but no control over which classes touch. Plurigaussian simulation truncates latent Gaussian fields with a truncation rule. With one field and ordered thresholds (truncated Gaussian), class 1 can touch class 2, but class 1 never touches class 3. With two fields the rule can encode any contact pattern.

Optimization

Simulated annealing starts from a random image with the target histogram and swaps pairs of values, accepting swaps that improve an objective (variogram mismatch, correlation with a secondary) under a cooling schedule. It can match almost any statistic you can write down, but it is not sampling from a defined probability model, so the spread between realizations is not a calibrated uncertainty.

Co-simulation

A secondary variable known everywhere (seismic, a DEM, a remote-sensing index) enters through GaussianCoSimulator (collocated cokriging), IndicatorCoSimulator (soft indicators), or DirectSequentialSimulator (collocated cokriging in original units). sgcosim_hierarchical simulates two variables jointly when both are known only at the samples.

Choosing a method

Situation Method Why

Large regular grid FFTSimulator Exact and \(O(N \log N)\)
Large irregular set of points TBSimulator Linear in the number of points
Original-unit variogram must be reproduced (skewed variable) DirectSequentialSimulator No normal-score transform of the field
Highs and lows have different continuity IndicatorCoSimulator / sisim One indicator variogram per threshold
Facies with ordered or restricted contacts PluriGaussianSimulator The truncation rule controls contacts
Facies without contact rules IndicatorCoSimulator (categorical) One indicator variogram per class
A secondary variable known everywhere GaussianCoSimulator, IndicatorCoSimulator, DirectSequentialSimulator with a secondary Collocated cokriging or soft data

Simulation in GeoLattice

The variogram list

The simulators share GSLIB’s variogram convention, a list

vario = [azimuth, nugget, major_range, minor_range, sill, vtype]   # plus smoothness for "matern"
  • azimuth is in degrees, major_range and minor_range are practical ranges (the distance at which the model reaches about 95 % of its sill), and sill is the total \(C(0)\) = nugget + partial sill.
  • vtype is "spherical", "exponential", "gaussian" or "matern" (Matérn needs a 7th element, the smoothness).
  • For Gaussian methods with itrans=True the variogram is that of the normal scores, so its sill is 1.
  • DirectSequentialSimulator and SimulatedAnnealing use the variogram in original units.

The call pattern

The functions follow GSLIB:

sims = sgsim(grid, df, "X", "Y", "Z", num_points=16, vario=vario, radius=500, nsim=50, itrans=True, seed=1)

The classes fit once and simulate many times:

sim = LUSimulator(vario, kriging_type="ordinary", itrans=True).fit(grid, df, "X", "Y", "Z")
sims = sim.simulate(nsim=200, seed=1)            # shape (nsim, len(grid))

grid is an (n, 2) array of node coordinates and df a DataFrame with the samples. The output always has one row per realization, in grid order. The same seed gives the same realizations. itrans=True normal-scores the data and back-transforms the realizations, and zmin / zmax bound the back-transform’s tails.

GPU acceleration

Simulator use_gpu When it pays off
GaussianCoSimulator, sgsim_gpu one CUDA thread per realization from about 16 realizations; about 100× at 1 000
IndicatorCoSimulator one CUDA thread per realization from about 16 realizations; about 110× at 1 000
DirectSequentialSimulator one thread (Soares) or one block (lookup) per realization from about 16 realizations
TBSimulator line processes and conditioning on the GPU from a single realization; 25-100×
sgsim, sisim batched local systems little gain; their octant search dominates

GPU realizations are identical to the CPU ones for the same seed, and every simulator falls back to the CPU (with a warning) when no GPU is usable. This notebook runs on the CPU so it gives the same numbers on any machine.

A synthetic truth and 60 samples

Every simulator is judged here against a field whose truth is known. The field is built from a Gaussian random field \(Y\) with a spherical variogram (practical ranges 400 m along 30° and 200 m across, sill 1), drawn on a 50 × 50 grid of 20 m cells. The variable of interest is lognormal, like a metal concentration:

\[ Z = \exp(0.8\,Y + 3). \]

Sixty grid nodes are sampled at random. Because \(Y\) is a monotone transform of \(Z\), the normal scores of the samples follow the same variogram as \(Y\). All the Gaussian simulators below therefore use the variogram that generated the truth, so the comparison is between simulation methods, not between variogram fits. (Fitting is the subject of the variogram notebook.)

Code

Kriging versus simulation

LUSimulator gives both objects from one fit: its conditional_mean_ is the ordinary-kriging estimate of the normal scores, and simulate draws exact conditional realizations from the same covariance. The kriged normal scores are back-transformed for display. Below, the truth, the kriging map, and one realization are compared on three counts: the map, the histogram, and the variogram of \(\log Z\) (computed along the grid rows and columns).

Code

def grid_variogram(values, max_lag=15):
    """Experimental semivariogram along grid rows and columns, lags 1..max_lag cells."""
    img = np.asarray(values).reshape(NX, NX)
    gam = []
    for h in range(1, max_lag + 1):
        d = np.concatenate([(img[h:, :] - img[:-h, :]).ravel(), (img[:, h:] - img[:, :-h]).ravel()])
        gam.append(0.5 * np.mean(d ** 2))
    return np.arange(1, max_lag + 1) * DX, np.array(gam)

That is expected: a conditional realization equals the kriging estimate plus a simulated error with the kriging variance, so its squared error is about twice kriging’s.

So the two answer different questions. Use the kriging map for the best value at a location. Use realizations for anything that depends on the spatial pattern: extremes, areas, connectivity.

The realization’s histogram has narrow peaks. These are not noise. With itrans=True the back-transform interpolates between the 60 sample quantiles, so every realization inherits the sample histogram’s clusters and gaps. In a real study the reference histogram is usually declustered and smoothed, or extended with tail models, before simulation.

One realization from every method

Each simulator now produces one realization from the same 60 samples. The Gaussian methods use the normal-score variogram above with itrans=True. DirectSequentialSimulator and SimulatedAnnealing work in original units, so they get the same model shape with the sill set to the sample variance of \(Z\). sisim uses nine thresholds, the sample deciles, with one indicator variogram for all of them (a dict gives each threshold its own). GaussianCoSimulator is run twice: alone, and with a secondary variable known at every node. The secondary is the true \(Y\) blurred by an independent smooth field, a stand-in for something like a geophysical survey.

The table records the run time, whether the samples are honoured (the largest difference at the 60 data nodes), the mean and standard deviation of the realization, and the correlation of \(\log Z\) with the truth.

  • Simulated annealing reproduces the histogram best (mean 36.3, standard deviation 27.9, the samples’ own values, since it only rearranges them). It has the lowest correlation with the truth, 0.17.   • Indicator simulation with one variogram for all nine thresholds gives a patchy texture: values inside a class band are drawn by interpolation, so neighbouring cells often share a narrow range. Its correlation with the truth is 0.22. Indicator methods earn their place when the low and high values have different continuity, which this Gaussian truth does not have.

Categorical simulation: indicators versus plurigaussian

Many variables are classes, not numbers: facies, soil types, land cover. Here the truth is cut into three ordered facies by thresholds on the latent field, low (\(Y < -0.5\)), middle, and high (\(Y > 0.6\)). Because the latent field is continuous, the low and high facies are separated by the middle one. On a 20 m grid they meet only where the field happens to jump across the whole middle band between neighbouring cells, which is rare.

  • IndicatorCoSimulator (categorical sequential indicator simulation) gives each facies an indicator variogram but has no notion of order.
  • PluriGaussianSimulator with a truncated-Gaussian rule (TruncationRule.truncated_gaussian) simulates the latent field and cuts it at thresholds set from the facies proportions of the samples. The rule puts the middle facies between the other two, as in the truth.

Both honour the facies at the samples and both take their proportions from the samples. The check that separates them is the number of grid edges where a low cell sits directly beside a high one.

def low_high_contacts(f):
    img = np.asarray(f).reshape(NX, NX)
    pairs = [(img[1:, :], img[:-1, :]), (img[:, 1:], img[:, :-1])]
    return int(sum((((a == 0) & (b == 2)) | ((a == 2) & (b == 0))).sum() for a, b in pairs))

From realizations to decisions

One realization is one plausible field; the information is in the set. LUSimulator has already factorized the covariance, so 500 more realizations cost a fraction of a second. Three summaries are mapped from them:

  • the E-type map, the node-by-node mean of the realizations, which converges to the conditional expectation;
  • the conditional standard deviation, small near the samples and large between them;
  • the probability that \(Z\) exceeds a threshold \(z_c\), the fraction of realizations above it at each node.

The last line answers a question kriging alone cannot: the probability that more than 15 % of the area exceeds \(z_c\). That is a property of whole maps, so it can only be read from the realizations.

The conditional standard deviation is zero at the samples and grows between them. It is largest in the hot spot, because a lognormal variable’s uncertainty grows with its value (the proportional effect), which a single Gaussian kriging variance cannot show in original units.
  • Calibration. The truth falls inside E-type ± 1.645 standard deviations at 89.2 % of the nodes, against a nominal 90 %. The spread is honest.
  • Area above the threshold. 18.6 % of the true field exceeds \(z_c = 51\). The kriging map says 8.3 %, less than half: smoothing removes the highs. The probability that more than 15 % of the area exceeds the threshold is 0.81, a statement about the whole map that no kriging map or kriging variance can provide.

The 500 realizations took 0.10 s because LUSimulator reuses its factorization. On larger grids FFTSimulator or TBSimulator play that role, and the sequential simulators can use the GPU.
